U1-262 marks

When is GM preferred to AM?

Answer

GM measures the actual compound growth over several periods (past performance); AM is the best estimate of a single future period's return. GM ≤ AM always.

U1-272 marks

Why is standard deviation used as a measure of risk?

Answer

It measures the dispersion of returns around the mean: the larger the spread, the less certain the return. It captures total risk (systematic + unsystematic).

Describe the structure of the Indian securities market and the process of trading and settlement. (10)

U4-3410 marks

Explain the dividend discount models of equity valuation with their assumptions and limitations. (10)

Answer

Intrinsic value = PV of dividends. (1) Zero-growth: P = D/k (preference shares, mature firms). (2) Constant growth (Gordon): P = D1/(k − g); assumptions: constant g forever, k > g, constant payout and ROE. (3) Multi-stage: supernormal growth phase then stable growth; terminal price by Gordon. Limitations: firms that pay no dividends (use FCFE); sensitivity to k − g; constant-growth unrealistic; estimating g and k is subjective. Show one worked example.

U5-415 marks

Explain the Markowitz portfolio theory and the efficient frontier with a diagram.

Answer

Markowitz (1952): portfolio risk depends on weights, variances and covariances; combining imperfectly correlated assets reduces risk. The feasible set of portfolios in risk–return space has an upper-left boundary: the efficient frontier — highest return for each risk. The investor picks the point where the highest indifference curve touches the frontier. Adding Rf gives the CML tangent at the market portfolio.

U5-3710 marks

Explain the methods of evaluating portfolio performance. Why do rankings differ? (10)

Answer

Sharpe (excess return/σ), Treynor (excess return/β), Jensen's alpha (Rp − CAPM return), M² (risk-adjusted return at market risk), information ratio (active return/tracking error). Rankings differ because Sharpe uses total risk and Treynor systematic risk (a poorly diversified fund ranks lower on Sharpe), and Jensen measures absolute rather than per-unit excess return (favours high-beta funds). Use Sharpe for a sole investment, Treynor/Jensen for a component of a diversified portfolio.
