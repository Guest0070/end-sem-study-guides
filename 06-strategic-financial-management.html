Unit I · 12 hoursFinancial Planning and Evaluation of Risky Proposals for Investment Decisions

This is the largest unit (12 of 45 teaching hours) and your CIA 1 used exactly its tools (RADR, sensitivity, scenario, decision tree). Expect it in Section A as theory (planning process, interface, sustainable strategies, risk vs uncertainty) or as a numerical (CE, RADR, ENPV with probability), and very often as the 20-mark case. The unit has two halves: strategy (what the firm wants and how finance supports it) and risk analysis (how to decide on an investment whose cash flows are uncertain).

1.1 Strategic financial management: meaning, features and scope

Ordinary financial management asks "Is this machine worth buying?" Strategic financial management asks "Given where we want the company to be in ten years, which investments, which sources of money and which payout policy get us there with acceptable risk?" It is financial management with a long-term, whole-company, competitive lens.

Strategic Financial Management (SFM) is the application of financial management techniques to the long-term strategic objectives of the organisation: identifying, evaluating and implementing investment, financing and dividend strategies (together with the management of working capital and risk) so as to maximise the long-run value of the firm for its shareholders while remaining consistent with its mission and stakeholders' interests.

Your course description adds the practical emphasis: applying financial techniques strategically to achieve organisational objectives, choosing appropriate sources of finance for an optimum capital structure, and managing financial resources through better investment decisions so as to maximise value.

Features of SFM

  1. Long-term orientation — horizon of 5–10 years or more; decisions are hard to reverse (a steel plant, a fleet of aircraft).
  2. Value focus — the objective is wealth (NPV, EVA, share value), not accounting profit of one year.
  3. Whole-firm view — decisions are taken with the portfolio of businesses in mind, not department by department.
  4. Integration with corporate strategy — finance follows and also shapes the business strategy (growth, diversification, turnaround).
  5. Explicit handling of risk and uncertainty — probabilities, scenarios, real options.
  6. Environment-sensitive — interest rates, regulation (RBI, SEBI), taxation, competition and ESG expectations are built into plans.
  7. Flexibility — plans are revised through feedback and control; options to expand or abandon are valued.

Every strategy eventually needs money and must earn more than the money costs. SFM is the discipline that checks this before the money is committed and measures it afterwards.

Reliance Industries funded its move into telecom (Jio) and retail with a mix of internal cash, debt and, in 2020, a ₹53,124 crore rights issue plus stake sales in Jio Platforms to strategic investors — a financing strategy chosen to fit a growth strategy and to reduce debt.

Traditional financial management (as taught in Semester III/IV) is mostly techniques: NPV, cost of capital, ratios. SFM uses the same techniques but asks why and for what strategic purpose. In an answer, always connect a technique to a strategic objective.

1.2 Formulation of strategies and policies, objectives and goals

Think of a cricket team. The mission is "be the best Test side". An objective is "win the series against Australia". A goal is "score 350+ in the first innings". The strategy is "play five bowlers and bat deep". A policy is "no batter plays a hook shot in the first 10 overs". Finance has the same hierarchy.

How financial strategies and policies are formulated (steps)

  1. Start from the corporate mission and objectives (value maximisation, subject to stakeholder and sustainability constraints).
  2. Translate objectives into measurable financial goals (growth in EPS, ROE, EVA, credit rating, payout).
  3. Assess internal financial position (cash generation, leverage, cost of capital) and the external environment (interest rates, capital-market conditions, regulation, tax).
  4. Generate alternative financial strategies (e.g., organic growth financed by retained earnings vs acquisition financed by debt).
  5. Evaluate them with SFM tools (NPV under risk, financial projections, ratio targets, EVA).
  6. Select and convert the strategy into policies (capital structure policy, dividend policy, WC policy, hedging policy).
  7. Implement through budgets, and monitor with feedback and control.

Infosys publishes a capital allocation policy that commits to return a large share (around 85%) of its free cash flow to shareholders over a multi-year period through dividends and buybacks — a policy derived from the objective of shareholder value and the fact that a cash-rich IT services firm has limited capital-intensive investment needs.

  1. Classify: "Keep debt-equity below 0.5" — goal or policy?
    AnswerIt is a policy (a standing rule governing financing decisions). If phrased "reduce debt-equity from 0.9 to 0.5 by March 2028", it is a goal.
  2. Why must goals be measurable?
    AnswerBecause the control stage of strategic planning compares actual with planned performance; without numbers there is nothing to compare.

1.3 Components of financial strategy

Financial strategy is the long-term plan for acquiring, allocating and managing the firm's financial resources — investment, financing, dividend, liquidity and risk decisions — so that they support the corporate strategy and maximise value.

  1. Investment (capital allocation) strategy — which projects, capacities, acquisitions and R&D to fund; hurdle rates adjusted for risk (RADR); portfolio balance between core and new businesses.
  2. Financing strategy — sources (equity, retained earnings, debt, hybrid, leasing, HP), capital structure target, maturity matching, cost of capital minimisation, credit rating goals.
  3. Dividend and retention strategy — payout ratio, stability, buybacks, bonus issues; signalling to the market.
  4. Working capital and liquidity strategy — levels of cash, receivables and inventory; short-term sources; conservative vs aggressive stance.
  5. Risk management strategy — business, financial, currency, interest-rate and commodity risk; hedging with derivatives; insurance.
  6. Profit-planning and cost strategy — margin targets, cost leadership, budgeting.
  7. Value and performance measurement — ROI, ROE, EVA, MVA used as targets and for managerial incentives.
  8. Sustainability/ESG strategy — green finance, ESG-linked borrowing, disclosure (SEBI BRSR).
  9. Restructuring strategy — mergers, demergers, divestments, turnaround financing.

For 5 marks, write any five components with a one-line explanation each plus one Indian example (e.g., Tata Steel's debt-reduction focus after the Corus acquisition is a financing-strategy component; TCS's buybacks are a dividend/distribution component).

1.4 Strategic planning process

Strategic (financial) planning is the systematic process of setting long-term objectives, analysing the environment and the firm's resources, formulating and evaluating alternative strategies, translating the chosen strategy into financial plans and budgets, and implementing and controlling them through feedback.

  1. Define mission and financial objectives — e.g., shareholder wealth maximisation with a credit-rating floor.
  2. Environmental analysis — PESTEL (political, economic, social, technological, environmental, legal) and industry forces; opportunities and threats.
  3. Internal resource analysis — strengths and weaknesses: cash-generating capacity, debt capacity, cost of capital, skills.
  4. Formulate alternative strategies — expansion, diversification, acquisition, divestment, cost leadership.
  5. Evaluate and choose — NPV/IRR with risk analysis (CE, RADR, sensitivity, scenario, decision tree), strategic fit, funding feasibility.
  6. Prepare the financial plan — projected statements, capital budget, funding plan, cash budget, policies (dividend, WC, hedging).
  7. Implement — raise funds, commit capital, allocate responsibility.
  8. Monitor, control and feedback — variance analysis, post-completion audit, performance measures (ROI, EVA); revise the plan.

The process forces consistency: a growth strategy with no funding plan, or a funding plan that breaks the dividend policy, is caught before money is committed.

TCS sets long-term goals (margin band, cash conversion), invests organically with little debt, and returns surplus cash through dividends and periodic buybacks; the annual report then reports performance against those financial priorities — a complete plan-implement-review loop.

1.5 Interface between corporate strategy and financing strategy

Corporate strategy decides where the company is going; financing strategy decides how to pay for the journey. A car-loan suited to a short trip is wrong for a 20-year journey — maturity, risk and flexibility of the money must match the strategy.

The interface between corporate strategy and financing strategy is the two-way relationship in which the corporate strategy (growth, diversification, acquisition, stability, retrenchment) determines the amount, timing, risk and maturity of funds required, while the availability and cost of finance (capital structure, market conditions, credit rating) constrain and shape which corporate strategies are feasible.

Points of interface (write these in an answer)

  1. Funds requirement — the strategy sets the size and timing of capital needs.
  2. Risk matching — business risk and financial risk should be balanced (high business risk → low leverage).
  3. Maturity matching — long-term assets financed with long-term funds.
  4. Cost of capital as hurdle — financing determines WACC, which is the minimum return every strategy must beat.
  5. Control and ownership — equity dilutes control; debt imposes covenants; the promoter's appetite shapes both.
  6. Flexibility — unused debt capacity is a strategic option for future opportunities.
  7. Life-cycle fit — start-up (equity/VC) → growth (equity + some debt) → maturity (debt + high payout) → decline (return of capital).
  8. Signalling — financing choices (buyback, rights issue) send information to investors.

Tata Steel's 2007 acquisition of Corus was financed largely with debt raised overseas. The corporate strategy (global scale) was bold; the financing strategy left the group heavily leveraged when European steel demand weakened, and much of the following decade was spent on deleveraging and restructuring the European operations — a textbook illustration of why corporate and financing strategies must be designed together.

Reliance Industries: its consumer-business growth (Jio, Retail) was financed partly by selling minority stakes to strategic and financial investors and by a rights issue in 2020, bringing down net debt — a financing strategy that supported, rather than limited, the corporate strategy.

1.6 Sustainable financial strategies

Sustainable financial strategies are long-term financing and investment strategies that integrate environmental, social and governance (ESG) considerations into capital allocation, funding and risk management, so that the firm creates value for shareholders while meeting its obligations to society and the environment and remaining financially resilient over the long run.

"Sustainable" has two meanings in exam answers, and a good answer mentions both:

  1. Financially sustainable — growth that the firm can fund without excessive leverage (the sustainable growth rate g = ROE × b, where b is the retention ratio).
  2. ESG-sustainable — strategies that respect environmental and social limits, which reduces long-term risks (carbon taxes, regulation, reputational loss) and widens access to capital.

Instruments and practices

  • Green bonds — proceeds used only for eligible green projects (renewables, clean transport). The Government of India issued its first sovereign green bonds in 2023; SEBI has a framework for green debt securities.
  • Sustainability-linked loans/bonds — interest rate steps up or down depending on ESG targets (e.g., emission intensity).
  • ESG-screened capital budgeting — internal carbon price added to project cash flows; projects must pass financial and ESG hurdles.
  • Disclosure — SEBI's Business Responsibility and Sustainability Report (BRSR), mandatory for the top 1,000 listed companies by market capitalisation from FY2022-23.
  • Stakeholder-based dividend policy — balancing payout with reinvestment in sustainability.

1.7 Risk and uncertainty in capital budgeting

Risk is rolling a fair die: you do not know the outcome, but you know each face has a 1/6 chance. Uncertainty is being asked to bet on a game whose rules you have never seen: you cannot even assign reliable probabilities.

Risk is the variability of actual returns (cash flows) from expected returns where the possible outcomes and their probabilities can be estimated (objectively or subjectively). Uncertainty is a situation in which the possible outcomes and/or their probabilities cannot be estimated reliably. (Frank Knight's distinction.)

BasisRiskUncertainty
ProbabilitiesKnown or estimableNot known / not estimable
MeasurementMeasurable (SD, variance, CV, beta)Not measurable statistically

ExampleDemand for an FMCG product in a known marketReturns from an unproven technology such as green hydrogen at scale

Sources of project risk

  • Project-specific — estimation errors in cost, demand, execution delays.
  • Competitive — rivals' actions affect prices and volumes.
  • Industry-specific — technology change, regulation of that industry.
  • Market (systematic) — interest rates, inflation, GDP cycles; cannot be diversified away.
  • International — exchange-rate and political risk (e.g., Tata Steel's European operations).

Only market risk is priced by diversified shareholders (CAPM), which is why RADR can be set with beta. The firm, however, also cares about total project risk because of distress costs — which is why SD of NPV, sensitivity and scenarios are used.

1.9 Certainty equivalent (CE) approach

Would you rather have an uncertain ₹1,00,000 next year or a guaranteed ₹85,000? If you are indifferent, ₹85,000 is the certainty equivalent of the risky ₹1,00,000, and the CE coefficient is 0.85. The CE method replaces every risky cash flow with its "safe" equivalent, then discounts at the risk-free rate because no risk is left.

The certainty equivalent approach adjusts the expected (risky) cash flows of a project downward by certainty-equivalent coefficients (αt) — the ratio of the riskless cash flow the decision maker would accept to the risky expected cash flow — and discounts the resulting certain cash flows at the risk-free rate.

αt = Certain cash flowt / Risky (expected) cash flowt    (0 ≤ α ≤ 1)
NPV = Σ αt × CFt / (1 + rf)t − I0

αt usually falls as t increases (distant flows are riskier). rf = risk-free rate (e.g., government security yield). Link with RADR: αt = (1 + rf)t / (1 + k)t makes the two methods give the same NPV.

1.10 Risk-adjusted discount rate (RADR)

A bank charges a riskier borrower a higher interest rate. RADR does the same with projects: the riskier the project, the higher the rate used to discount its cash flows, so the same rupees are worth less today.

The risk-adjusted discount rate approach discounts a project's expected cash flows at a rate equal to the risk-free rate plus a risk premium that reflects the project's risk; the more risky the project, the higher the discount rate.

k = rf + risk premium     NPV = Σ CFt/(1 + k)t − I0
CAPM-based RADR: k = rf + βproject (Rm − rf)

rf = risk-free rate; βproject = systematic risk of the project (often a "pure-play" comparable company's beta); Rm = expected market return; (Rm − rf) = market risk premium. Risk premium can also come from a risk class table (low / normal / high risk → +2%, +5%, +8%).

Worked example 1 — risk-premium RADR

Cost ₹10,00,000; ₹3,50,000 a year for 5 years; rf = 8%; the project's risk class carries a 6% premium.

  1. k = 8% + 6% = 14%; 5-year annuity factor at 14% = 3.4332.
  2. PV = 3,50,000 × 3.4332 = ₹12,01,620; NPV = ₹2,01,620 → accept.
  3. At 8% (ignoring risk) NPV would be 3,50,000 × 3.9926 − 10,00,000 = ₹3,97,410 — nearly double.

Worked example 2 — CAPM RADR (Tata Steel-type expansion)

A downstream steel project costs ₹22,00,000 with cash flows ₹6,00,000, ₹8,00,000, ₹9,00,000, ₹7,00,000. Company WACC 10%. Project beta 1.4; rf 7%; Rm 12%.

  1. k = 7% + 1.4 × (12% − 7%) = 14%.
  2. PV at 14% = 6,00,000 × 0.8772 + 8,00,000 × 0.7695 + 9,00,000 × 0.6750 + 7,00,000 × 0.5921 = 5,26,320 + 6,15,600 + 6,07,500 + 4,14,470 = ₹21,63,890.
  3. NPV at 14% = 21,63,890 − 22,00,000 = −₹36,110 → reject.
  4. At WACC 10%: PV = 5,45,460 + 6,61,120 + 6,76,170 + 4,78,100 = ₹23,60,850; NPV = +₹1,60,850.

Interpretation: using the company-wide WACC for a project riskier than the company's average accepts a value-destroying project. This is the single most important lesson of RADR.

1.11 Expected NPV (probability approach)

Expected NPV (ENPV) is the probability-weighted average of the NPVs of all possible outcomes; equivalently, the present value of the expected cash flows of each year less the investment.

Expected CFt = Σ pi × CFit     ENPV = Σt E(CFt) / (1 + r)t − I0

pi = probability of outcome i (probabilities in each year sum to 1). The discount rate r is normally the risk-free rate when you then measure risk separately through SD (to avoid double counting); if the question gives a cost of capital, use it and say so.

Worked example 1 — same distribution every year

Cost ₹1,00,000; life 3 years; each year the cash flow is ₹30,000 (p = 0.3), ₹50,000 (p = 0.4) or ₹70,000 (p = 0.3); r = 10%.

  1. E(CF) = 30,000 × 0.3 + 50,000 × 0.4 + 70,000 × 0.3 = 9,000 + 20,000 + 21,000 = ₹50,000 per year.
  2. 3-year annuity factor at 10% = 0.9091 + 0.8264 + 0.7513 = 2.4868.
  3. ENPV = 50,000 × 2.4868 − 1,00,000 = ₹24,340.

1.12 Standard deviation of NPV and coefficient of variation

Two projects can have the same expected NPV but very different spreads. SD tells you how far the actual NPV is likely to land from the expected NPV. If cash flows of different years are independent, good and bad years partly cancel, so the SD is smaller; if they are perfectly correlated (a good year 1 means good later years too), there is no cancelling and the SD is larger.

σt2 = Σ pi(CFit − E(CFt))2    (variance of year-t cash flow)
Independent cash flows: σNPV = √[ Σ σt2 / (1 + r)2t ]
Perfectly correlated cash flows: σNPV = Σ σt / (1 + r)t
Coefficient of variation CV = σNPV / ENPV    (risk per rupee of expected NPV)

Note the square: in the independent case each variance is discounted by (1 + r)2t. Factors 1/(1.10)2t for t = 1, 2, 3: 0.8264, 0.6830, 0.5645.

Worked example 1 (continuing ENPV example 1)

  1. Variance each year: 0.3(30,000 − 50,000)² + 0.4(0)² + 0.3(70,000 − 50,000)² = 0.3 × 40,00,00,000 × 2 = 24,00,00,000; σt = ₹15,491.93.
  2. Independent: σ²NPV = 24,00,00,000 × (0.8264 + 0.6830 + 0.5645) = 24,00,00,000 × 2.0739 = 49,77,36,000; σNPV = ₹22,310.
  3. Perfectly correlated: σNPV = 15,491.93 × 2.4868 = ₹38,525.
  4. CV (independent) = 22,310 / 24,340 = 0.92; CV (correlated) = 38,525 / 24,340 = 1.58.

Interpretation: the correlated project is much riskier; the ENPV is the same in both cases.

1.13 Use of the normal distribution

If NPV is approximately normally distributed with mean ENPV and standard deviation σNPV, the probability of any NPV range is found by converting the boundary to a standard normal variable Z and reading the area from the Z table.

Z = (X − ENPV) / σNPV

P(NPV < 0): Z = (0 − ENPV)/σ, read the area to the left. P(NPV > X): 1 − area to the left of Z. P(A < NPV < B) = area(ZB) − area(ZA). Useful table values: area left of Z = 0.5 → 0.6915; 1.0 → 0.8413; 1.25 → 0.8944; 1.5 → 0.9332; 1.645 → 0.9500; 2.0 → 0.9772; negative Z: area = 1 − area(|Z|). Rule of thumb: ±1σ covers 68.3%, ±2σ 95.4%, ±3σ 99.7%.

Worked example 1 — probability of a loss and of a target

ENPV ₹24,340; σNPV ₹22,310 (independent case above).

  1. P(NPV < 0): Z = (0 − 24,340)/22,310 = −1.09; area to the left = 1 − 0.8621 = 0.1379 (13.79%).
  2. P(0 < NPV < 50,000): Z50,000 = (50,000 − 24,340)/22,310 = 1.15 → area 0.8749. Probability = 0.8749 − 0.1379 = 0.7370 (73.70%).
  3. P(NPV > 50,000) = 1 − 0.8749 = 0.1251 (12.51%).

Worked example 2 — risky project (ENPV example 2)

ENPV ₹90,262; σ ₹2,40,815. Z for zero = −90,262/2,40,815 = −0.37 → P(NPV < 0) = 1 − 0.6443 = 0.3557 (35.57%). P(NPV > ₹3,00,000): Z = (3,00,000 − 90,262)/2,40,815 = 0.87 → 1 − 0.8078 = 0.1922 (19.22%). Three common slips: (1) forgetting the minus sign in Z for P(NPV<0); (2) reading the table value 0.8621 as the answer instead of 1 − 0.8621; (3) using the correlated SD when the question says cash flows are independent.

1.14 Decision tree analysis

Some investments are a sequence of decisions: "test the market first, and launch only if the test is good." A decision tree draws every decision and chance event in time order, then works backwards ("roll-back"), choosing the best branch at each decision and averaging at each chance point.

Decision tree analysis is a graphical technique for sequential investment decisions under risk, in which decision points (squares) and chance events (circles) with their probabilities and payoffs are laid out as branches; the optimal strategy is found by rolling back from the end, taking expected values at chance nodes and the best value at decision nodes.

Steps

  1. Define the decision problem and time horizon.
  2. Draw the tree: squares = decisions, circles = chance events, branches = alternatives/outcomes.
  3. Assign probabilities (conditional where relevant) and cash flows (or PVs) to each branch.
  4. Compute the payoff (NPV) at the end of each path.
  5. Roll back: at a chance node take the probability-weighted average; at a decision node choose the highest value and "prune" (∥) the other branches.
  6. State the optimal strategy and its expected NPV; comment on risk (spread of outcomes).

Worked example 1 — test market or launch now (HUL-type product launch; ₹ lakh, values already in PV terms)

Launching nationally costs ₹50 lakh. Demand is high (PV of inflows ₹120 lakh) or low (₹30 lakh) with equal probability. Alternatively HUL can spend ₹2 lakh on a test market: the test is positive with probability 0.6 (then P(high) = 0.75) or negative with probability 0.4 (then P(high) = 0.125). After the test HUL decides whether to launch.

  1. Launch now: EV = 0.5 × 120 + 0.5 × 30 = 75; NPV = 75 − 50 = 25.
  2. After a positive test: launch EV = 0.75 × 120 + 0.25 × 30 = 97.5; NPV = 47.5 > 0 → launch; node value 47.5.
  3. After a negative test: launch EV = 0.125 × 120 + 0.875 × 30 = 41.25; NPV = −8.75 < 0 → abandon; node value 0.
  4. Test route: 0.6 × 47.5 + 0.4 × 0 − 2 = 28.5 − 2 = 26.5.
  5. Optimal strategy: test market first; launch only if the test is positive. Expected NPV ₹26.5 lakh. The test information is worth 28.5 − 25 = ₹3.5 lakh, more than its ₹2 lakh cost.

Consistency check: overall P(high) = 0.6 × 0.75 + 0.4 × 0.125 = 0.5, matching the launch-now branch.

1.15 Sensitivity analysis

Sensitivity analysis measures how much the NPV changes when one input variable (sales volume, price, variable cost, fixed cost, investment, life, discount rate) changes while all other variables are held constant; in its break-even form it finds the percentage change in each variable that would reduce NPV to zero.

Sensitivity (%) = NPV / PV of the cash flows affected by the variable × 100

Smallest % = most sensitive (critical) variable. For a variable that affects the annual cash flow, the allowable fall in annual cash flow = NPV ÷ annuity factor; convert this rupee amount into the change in that variable.

1.17 Monte Carlo simulation

Instead of three scenarios, let a computer play out the project thousands of times, each time drawing random values of demand, price and cost from their probability distributions. The thousands of NPVs form a distribution from which you read the mean, SD and probability of loss.

Monte Carlo simulation is a risk-analysis technique that builds a model of the project's NPV, assigns a probability distribution to each uncertain variable, repeatedly draws random values of those variables to compute NPV, and uses the resulting distribution of NPVs to assess expected value and risk.

Steps (write these for 5 marks)

  1. Build the model: NPV as a function of the key variables.
  2. Identify uncertain variables and specify each one's probability distribution (and correlations between them).
  3. Assign random-number ranges to the outcomes in proportion to their probabilities (cumulative probability method).
  4. Draw random numbers, pick the variable values, compute NPV — one trial.
  5. Repeat hundreds or thousands of times.
  6. Analyse the output: mean NPV, SD, histogram, P(NPV < 0).

1.18 Real options in capital budgeting

A traditional NPV assumes you invest today and then passively watch. Real managers react: they expand if demand booms, abandon if it collapses, or wait for better information. These choices are options — rights, not obligations — and options have value that static NPV misses.

A real option is the right, but not the obligation, to take a future business decision about a real asset (expand, abandon, delay, switch) after uncertainty is partly resolved. Strategic (expanded) NPV = static NPV + value of the real options.

Worked example 1 — value of an abandonment option (₹ lakh)

Cost 100. After one year the project will be worth (PV at t=1 of remaining flows) 140 or 60 with equal probability, plus a year-1 cash flow of 10 either way. At t=1 the assets can be sold for 80. r = 10%.

  1. Without the option: ENPV = [0.5(140 + 10) + 0.5(60 + 10)] / 1.10 − 100 = 110/1.10 − 100 = 0.
  2. With the option: in the bad state sell for 80 instead of keeping 60 → [0.5 × 150 + 0.5 × 90]/1.10 − 100 = 120/1.10 − 100 = 9.09.
  3. Value of the abandonment option = ₹9.09 lakh; the project becomes acceptable.

Worked example 2 — option to expand

Phase 1 of a project has static NPV −₹5 crore. If demand is high (p = 0.4), a phase-2 expansion in year 2 will have an NPV (valued at year 2) of ₹40 crore. r = 10%.

PV of expansion option = 0.4 × 40 / (1.10)² = 16/1.21 = ₹13.22 crore. Strategic NPV = −5 + 13.22 = ₹8.22 crore → accept phase 1 as a "strategic entry" investment.

Worked example 3 — option to delay

Invest ₹100 crore now for PV ₹110 crore (NPV 10). Or wait one year: demand becomes known — high (0.5, PV at t=1 of ₹150 crore) or low (0.5, PV ₹70 crore; then do not invest). Value of waiting = 0.5 × (150 − 100)/1.10 = ₹22.73 crore > ₹10 crore, so waiting is better (ignoring the risk that a competitor pre-empts).

Real options add value only when there is (1) uncertainty and (2) managerial flexibility to act on new information. An option you cannot exercise (no salvage market, contractual lock-in) is worth nothing.

Q1. Explain the strategic planning process in financial management. (5 marks)

Introduction. Strategic financial planning is the systematic process of setting long-term financial objectives, evaluating alternative strategies and converting the chosen strategy into financial plans that are implemented and controlled through feedback.

Steps.

  3. Internal appraisal — strengths/weaknesses in cash generation, debt capacity and cost of capital.

  5. Evaluation and choice — NPV with risk analysis (RADR, sensitivity, scenarios, decision trees) and strategic fit.

Q2. Discuss the interface between corporate strategy and financing strategy. (5 marks)

Introduction. Corporate strategy defines the businesses and growth path of the firm; financing strategy decides the amount, sources, cost, risk and maturity of the funds. The two are interdependent.

  1. Funds requirement: growth, diversification or acquisition strategies determine how much capital is needed and when.
  2. Risk balance: high business risk (new ventures) calls for low financial risk (equity); stable businesses can carry more debt.
  3. Maturity matching: long-lived assets are financed with long-term funds; seasonal needs with short-term funds.
  4. Cost of capital as hurdle: the financing mix sets WACC, which every strategy must beat.
  5. Control: equity dilutes promoters; debt brings covenants.
  6. Flexibility: unused debt capacity keeps strategic options open.
  7. Constraint: if finance is unavailable or too costly, the corporate strategy must change.

Example. Tata Steel's largely debt-financed acquisition of Corus (2007) left it highly leveraged when European demand weakened, forcing years of deleveraging — the corporate and financing strategies had not been sufficiently matched for downside risk.

Conclusion. Financing strategy should be designed alongside, not after, corporate strategy.

Q3. What are sustainable financial strategies? Illustrate with Indian companies. (5 marks)

Q4. Distinguish between risk and uncertainty and explain the certainty equivalent approach. (5 marks)

Risk vs uncertainty. Risk exists when outcomes and their probabilities can be estimated (measurable by SD, CV); uncertainty exists when they cannot (handled by sensitivity, scenario, judgement).

CE approach. Each expected cash flow is multiplied by a certainty-equivalent coefficient αt = certain CF / risky CF (0–1), converting it into a riskless amount; these are discounted at the risk-free rate: NPV = Σ αtCFt/(1 + rf)t − I0.

Illustration. Expected CF ₹2,00,000 in year 1 with α = 0.9 and rf = 6% gives PV = 1,80,000 × 0.9434 = ₹1,69,812.

Merits: separates time value and risk; allows different risk for each year. Limits: α is subjective; inconsistent across managers.

Conclusion. CE is theoretically superior to RADR but relies heavily on managerial judgement.

Q5. Explain decision tree analysis with its steps and merits. (5 marks)

Meaning. A graphic method for sequential investment decisions under risk showing decision nodes, chance nodes, probabilities and payoffs; solved by roll-back.

Unit II · 5 hoursDividend Theory and Policy

A short unit that examiners love because it gives crisp 5-mark numericals (Walter, Gordon, MM) and clean theory questions (determinants, assumptions, criticisms, comparison). The central question: can a company increase its share price by changing how much of its earnings it pays out? Walter and Gordon say yes (relevance); Modigliani and Miller say no (irrelevance) under perfect markets.

2.1 Meaning and importance of dividend policy

Every rupee of profit after tax can either go to shareholders now (dividend) or stay in the company to fund growth (retained earnings). Dividend policy is the rule the board uses to split that rupee — and the split matters because it affects growth, the need for outside finance, and what investors think about the company.

A dividend is the portion of a company's distributable profits paid to its shareholders as a return on their investment. Dividend policy is the policy a company follows in deciding the amount, form, timing and stability of dividends — i.e., the division of earnings between payout to shareholders and retention for reinvestment.

Key ratios: payout ratio = DPS / EPS; retention ratio b = 1 − payout; dividend yield = DPS / market price.

Importance

  1. Affects share value (relevance theories) and the wealth of shareholders.
  2. Source of finance — retained earnings are the cheapest and most convenient internal source; dividend policy is therefore also a financing decision.
  3. Signalling — a dividend increase signals management's confidence in future earnings.
  4. Clientele — retired investors want regular income; growth investors prefer retention.
  5. Control — high payout may force equity issues that dilute control.
  6. Liquidity — dividends are cash outflows; policy must respect the firm's cash position (a central theme of your CIA 2 simulation).

In India, dividends have been taxable in the hands of shareholders since 1 April 2020 (the earlier Dividend Distribution Tax paid by companies was abolished by the Finance Act, 2020). This changed the tax attractiveness of dividends versus buybacks for high-bracket investors — a live determinant of payout decisions.

2.6 Walter's model

Suppose the company can earn 15% on money it keeps, but you (the shareholder) can only earn 10% elsewhere. You would rather it kept all the profit. If the company earns only 8%, you would rather it paid everything out. If it earns exactly 10%, you do not care. That is Walter's model in three sentences.

Walter's model holds that dividend policy is relevant and depends on the relationship between the firm's internal rate of return (r) and its cost of equity (k): the market price of a share is the present value of the perpetual dividend stream plus the present value of the perpetual gains from retained earnings.

P = [ D + (r / k)(E − D) ] / k

P = market price per share; D = dividend per share; E = earnings per share; (E − D) = retained earnings per share; r = internal rate of return on investments; k = cost of equity (capitalisation rate). Written as P = D/k + [r(E − D)/k]/k.

Assumptions

  1. All financing is through retained earnings; no external debt or equity.
  2. r and k remain constant.
  3. All earnings are either distributed or reinvested immediately.
  4. E and D remain constant (perpetuity), and the firm has an infinite life.
  5. No taxes (or none that differ between dividends and capital gains).

2.7 Gordon's model (dividend capitalisation / bird-in-hand)

"A bird in the hand is worth two in the bush." Investors are risk-averse: a rupee of dividend today is certain; a rupee of future capital gain is uncertain. Gordon argues that retaining earnings makes the dividend stream more distant and risky, so investors raise their required return — and share price can fall even if r = k when uncertainty is considered.

Gordon's model values a share as the present value of an infinite stream of dividends growing at a constant rate g = b × r, where b is the retention ratio and r the return on retained earnings; dividend policy is relevant because it determines both the current dividend and the growth rate.

P0 = E1(1 − b) / (k − br)     equivalently P0 = D1 / (k − g), g = br

E1 = EPS (of the coming year); b = retention ratio; (1 − b) = payout ratio; r = return on investment; k = cost of equity; condition: k > br (otherwise the formula is meaningless). If only D0 is given: D1 = D0(1 + g).

Assumptions

  1. All-equity firm; retained earnings are the only source of finance.
  2. r and k are constant, and k > br.
  3. Constant retention ratio b, so growth g = br is constant.
  4. Perpetual life; no corporate taxes.

2.8 Modigliani–Miller (MM) dividend irrelevance

Imagine a pizza (the firm's value). Paying a dividend is like handing you a slice, but the remaining pizza in the box shrinks by exactly that slice. And if the firm now needs money for a project, it sells a new slice to a new investor at a fair price. Your total pizza (cash + value of your shares) is unchanged — so the dividend decision does not change your wealth.

The MM hypothesis states that, under perfect capital markets and a given investment policy, the value of the firm is determined solely by its earning power (investment decision) and not by how earnings are split between dividends and retention; any dividend paid is exactly offset by a fall in the share price or by the value of new shares issued.

Assumptions

  1. Perfect capital markets: rational investors, free information, no transaction or flotation costs, no single investor influences price.
  2. No taxes, or no difference between tax on dividends and capital gains.
  3. Fixed investment policy (the firm's investments are decided independently of dividends).
  4. Perfect certainty about future investments and profits (later relaxed).
P0 = (D1 + P1) / (1 + ke)  ⇒  P1 = P0(1 + ke) − D1
New shares to be issued: m = [ I − (E − nD1) ] / P1
Value of the firm: nP0 = [ (n + m)P1 − I + E ] / (1 + ke)

n = existing shares; m = new shares issued at the end of the year at P1; I = total new investment; E = total net income; nD1 = total dividend; ke = cost of equity. Because nD1 cancels out when m is substituted, value is the same for every dividend.

Arbitrage proof (in words)

  1. Start with the firm's value today: nP0 = (nD1 + nP1)/(1 + ke).
  2. Investment must be financed: I = (E − nD1) + mP1 ⇒ mP1 = I − E + nD1.
  3. Add and subtract mP1: nP0 = [nD1 + (n + m)P1 − mP1]/(1 + ke).
  4. Substitute step 2: nP0 = [nD1 + (n + m)P1 − I + E − nD1]/(1 + ke) = [(n + m)P1 − I + E]/(1 + ke).
  5. D1 has disappeared: value depends on E, I and ke only. The gain to shareholders from a dividend is exactly offset by the dilution from new shares (the arbitrage between dividend payment and external financing).

Q1. Explain the determinants of dividend policy. (5 marks)

Introduction. Dividend policy decides the split of earnings between payout and retention; it is shaped by several internal and external factors.

  1. Earnings and their stability — stable firms pay more (HUL).
  2. Investment opportunities — growth firms retain (Reliance).
  3. Liquidity — dividends require cash, not accounting profit.
  4. Access to capital markets and cost of external funds.
  5. Legal and contractual limits — Companies Act 2013 (dividend only out of profits), loan covenants; SEBI requires a dividend distribution policy for the top 1,000 listed entities.
  6. Shareholder preferences and taxation — clientele; dividends taxed in investors' hands since 2020.
  7. Control — retention avoids dilution.
  8. Signalling and stability — boards avoid cuts.

Conclusion. The optimal policy balances shareholder income needs, growth funding and liquidity; Infosys's formal capital-allocation policy shows how companies codify these trade-offs.

Q2. State and criticise Walter's model. (5 marks)

Model. P = [D + (r/k)(E − D)]/k — dividend policy is relevant and depends on r relative to k.

Assumptions. Only retained earnings finance investment; constant r and k; constant E and D; infinite life; all earnings paid or reinvested.

Implications. r > k → 0% payout; r = k → irrelevant; r < k → 100% payout. E.g., with E = ₹10, k = 10%, r = 15%, P = ₹150 at zero payout vs ₹100 at full payout.

Criticisms. No external financing; constant r ignores diminishing returns; constant k ignores changes in risk; ignores taxes and investor preference.

Conclusion. A useful first approximation linking payout to profitability, but too restrictive for real firms.

Q3. Explain the MM dividend irrelevance theory with its assumptions and limitations. (5 marks)

Proposition. Under perfect markets and a given investment policy, the value of a firm depends only on its earning power; dividend policy is irrelevant.

Assumptions. Perfect capital markets; no taxes; fixed investment policy; no flotation or transaction costs; certainty.

Mechanism. P0 = (D1 + P1)/(1 + ke): a dividend lowers P1 by the same amount; if funds are needed, new shares are sold at P1; value nP0 = [(n + m)P1 − I + E]/(1 + ke) does not contain D.

Illustration. 1,00,000 shares at ₹100, ke 10%, E ₹10 lakh, I ₹15 lakh: value = ₹1 crore with or without a ₹5 dividend.

Limitations. Taxes, flotation costs, transaction costs, information asymmetry (signalling), clientele effects.

Conclusion. Irrelevance holds only in a perfect market; real-world frictions make dividend policy matter.

3.1 Working capital concepts

A tea stall owner buys milk and leaves, makes tea, sells it, sometimes on credit to office regulars, and pays the milk supplier at the end of the week. The money stuck in milk, sugar and unpaid customer bills, minus what the supplier lets him owe, is his working capital.

Gross working capital is the firm's total investment in current assets (cash, marketable securities, receivables, inventory, prepaid expenses). Net working capital is current assets minus current liabilities. Working capital finance is the arrangement of funds — short-term and long-term — to support this investment.

3.2 Operating cycle (cash conversion cycle)

The operating cycle is the time taken to convert cash into raw materials, raw materials into work-in-progress and finished goods, finished goods into sales (receivables), and receivables back into cash. Gross operating cycle = RMCP + WIPCP + FGCP + DCP; net operating cycle (cash conversion cycle) = gross operating cycle − creditors' (payables) deferral period.

RMCP = Average RM stock / RM consumed per day    WIPCP = Average WIP / Cost of production per day

3.5 Cash management — motives and the optimum cash balance

Keynes's motives for holding cash: transaction (routine payments), precautionary (unexpected needs), speculative (to exploit opportunities); plus compensating balances required by banks. Holding too much cash loses interest (opportunity cost); holding too little causes frequent conversions (transaction cost) or shortages. The optimum balance minimises the total. Tools: cash budget, Baumol model (certain, steady outflows), Miller-Orr model (random flows), and techniques to speed collections (lock-box, electronic payments/UPI, concentration banking) and control disbursements.

3.6 Baumol model (EOQ applied to cash)

You spend ₹36 lakh steadily over a year and keep the rest invested at 12%. Each time you sell investments to top up cash, you pay ₹150. Sell rarely in big chunks → low transaction cost but lots of idle cash; sell often in small chunks → little idle cash but many fees. Baumol finds the chunk size that balances the two.

The Baumol model determines the optimum cash balance (the amount of marketable securities to convert into cash each time) that minimises the sum of the fixed cost of conversion and the opportunity cost of holding cash, assuming cash needs are known and used at a steady rate.

C* = √( 2TF / i )

T = total cash needed during the period; F = fixed cost per conversion (transaction); i = interest rate on marketable securities for the same period. Number of conversions = T/C*; average cash = C*/2; transaction cost = (T/C*) × F; opportunity cost = (C*/2) × i; at the optimum the two costs are equal.

Annual cash requirement ₹36,00,000; cost per conversion ₹150; interest on securities 12% a year.

  3. Average cash = ₹47,434.16. Transaction cost = 37.95 × 150 = ₹5,692.10; opportunity cost = 47,434.16 × 0.12 = ₹5,692.10; total = ₹11,384.20.

3.7 Miller-Orr model

The Miller-Orr model sets a lower limit (L), an upper limit (H) and a return point (Z) for the cash balance when daily cash flows are random. When cash reaches H, securities are bought to bring cash down to Z; when it falls to L, securities are sold to bring it back up to Z.

Spread = 3 × ( ¾ × F × σ2 / i )1/3
Return point = L + Spread / 3     Upper limit = L + Spread     Average cash ≈ L + (4/9) × Spread

F = cost per transaction; σ2 = variance of daily net cash flows; i = daily interest rate (annual ÷ 365 or 360); L = minimum balance set by management.

Worked example 1

Minimum balance ₹20,000; transaction cost ₹60; SD of daily cash flows ₹3,000 (variance 90,00,000); annual interest 9.125% → daily 0.025%.

  1. ¾ × 60 × 90,00,000 / 0.00025 = 16,20,00,00,00,000; cube root = 11,744.60.
  2. Spread = 3 × 11,744.60 = ₹35,233.81.
  3. Return point = 20,000 + 11,744.60 = ₹31,744.60; upper limit = 20,000 + 35,233.81 = ₹55,233.81; average cash ≈ ₹35,659.47.
  4. Policy: if cash hits ₹55,233.81, invest ₹23,489.21 (down to ₹31,744.60); if it falls to ₹20,000, sell ₹11,744.60 of securities.

3.8 Receivables management and credit-policy evaluation

Giving customers more time to pay sells more, but the extra sales come with costs: some customers never pay (bad debts), chasing them costs money, and the money locked up in unpaid bills could have earned interest. A credit policy is worth changing only if the extra contribution beats all three extra costs.

Receivables management is the process of deciding credit policy — credit standards, credit period, cash discount and collection policy — so as to maximise the net benefit (incremental contribution less incremental bad debts, collection costs and cost of funds tied up in receivables).

Net benefit = Contribution − Bad debts − Collection cost − Cost of investment in receivables (− cash discount)
Investment in receivables = Credit sales × (Variable cost / Sales) × (Collection period / 360)
Cost of investment = Investment in receivables × required rate of return

Cost basis (variable cost) is the standard approach because the firm has spent only the cost of goods; some texts use total cost or sales value — state the basis you use. Credit terms "2/10 net 30" = 2% discount if paid within 10 days, else full amount in 30 days.

3.10 Inventory: EOQ

Economic Order Quantity (EOQ) is the order size that minimises the total of ordering costs (which fall as order size rises) and carrying costs (which rise as order size rises), assuming constant demand, constant lead time and no discounts.

EOQ = √( 2AO / C )

A = annual demand (units); O = cost per order; C = carrying cost per unit per year (= price × carrying %). Number of orders = A/EOQ; ordering cost = (A/Q) × O; carrying cost = (Q/2) × C; total inventory cost = both; at EOQ the two are equal. Order interval = 360/number of orders.

Worked example 1

Annual demand 20,000 units; ordering cost ₹400 per order; price ₹80; carrying cost 20% of price (C = ₹16).

  1. EOQ = √(2 × 20,000 × 400 / 16) = √10,00,000 = 1,000 units.
  2. Orders a year = 20; order every 360/20 = 18 days.
  3. Ordering cost = 20 × 400 = ₹8,000; carrying cost = 500 × 16 = ₹8,000; total = ₹16,000.

Worked example 2 — reverse

EOQ is 600 units, annual demand 12,000 units and carrying cost ₹10 per unit. Ordering cost O = EOQ² × C / (2A) = 3,60,000 × 10 / 24,000 = ₹150 per order.

3.14 Integrating working capital and the capital investment process

A new plant needs not only machines but also stocks of raw material, finished goods and customer credit. This working capital is cash spent at the start (and as sales grow) that comes back at the end when stocks are run down and debtors pay. Forget it and the project looks better than it is.

Integration of working capital with capital budgeting means treating the incremental investment in net working capital required by a project as a cash outflow when it is needed (usually at the start and as sales grow) and as a cash inflow when it is released (usually at the end of the project life), so that NPV reflects the full capital commitment.

  • WC is usually not depreciated and has no direct tax effect; it is fully recovered at the end (unless stated otherwise).
  • Incremental WC each year = WC required for next year's sales − WC already in place.
  • Excess or inadequate inventory affects both liquidity and the project's NPV (course reflection question): excess stock ties up funds and lowers NPV; inadequate stock loses sales and contribution.

Worked example — the effect of WC on a decision

Project cost ₹50,00,000; annual cash inflow ₹15,00,000 for 5 years; r = 12% (AF 3.6048; PVF year 5 = 0.5674). The project also needs WC of ₹10,00,000 at the start, recovered at the end of year 5.

  1. NPV ignoring WC = 15,00,000 × 3.6048 − 50,00,000 = ₹4,07,200.
  2. With WC: NPV = 15,00,000 × 3.6048 + 10,00,000 × 0.5674 − 60,00,000 = 54,07,200 + 5,67,400 − 60,00,000 = −₹25,400.
  3. The PV cost of tying up WC = 10,00,000 − 5,67,400 = ₹4,32,600, which turns an acceptable project into a reject.

4.1 Leasing: meaning and importance

You need a car for three years. You can buy it (pay now, own it, claim depreciation, sell it later) or rent it long-term (pay a monthly rental, never own it, return it at the end). A lease is the business version of long-term renting: you get the use of an asset without owning it.

A lease is a contractual arrangement in which the owner of an asset (the lessor) grants another party (the lessee) the right to use the asset for an agreed period in return for periodic payments (lease rentals), while ownership remains with the lessor. Ind AS 116 defines a lease as a contract that conveys the right to control the use of an identified asset for a period of time in exchange for consideration.

Features

  1. Two parties — lessor (owner) and lessee (user); in leveraged leases a lender too.
  2. Separation of ownership and use.
  3. Periodic rentals (in advance or in arrears), fixed by contract.
  4. Lease term: primary period (non-cancellable) and possibly a secondary period at a nominal rent.
  5. Return, renewal or purchase option at the end.
  6. Tax: lessee deducts rentals; lessor, as owner, claims depreciation.

Importance / advantages to the lessee

  • 100% financing without a down payment; conserves cash and borrowing capacity for other uses.
  • Flexibility and protection against obsolescence (operating leases for IT equipment and aircraft).
  • Tax benefit — rentals fully deductible; useful when the lessee cannot use depreciation (losses).
  • Quick and simple documentation compared with term loans.
  • Maintenance often provided by the lessor (operating/full-service leases).
  • Asset-light strategy — higher asset turnover (important for airlines and retailers).

Advantages to the lessor

Earns steady rental income; retains ownership (security); claims depreciation; residual value upside; for manufacturers, leasing boosts sales.

Disadvantages

Lessee loses residual value; lease rentals can be costlier than borrowing; restrictive covenants; under Ind AS 116 leases now appear on the balance sheet, so the "off-balance-sheet" advantage has largely gone for lessees.

4.3 Tax considerations

  • Lessee: lease rentals are a deductible business expense; tax saving = rental × tax rate. The lessee does not claim depreciation on a leased asset.
  • Lessor: rentals are taxable income; as legal owner the lessor claims depreciation (the Supreme Court in ICDS Ltd. v. CIT (2013) upheld a lessor's depreciation claim on leased vehicles).
  • Hire purchase: the hirer claims depreciation on the full cash price and deducts only the interest portion of instalments (the principal is not an expense).
  • Tax-rate arbitrage: leasing adds value when the lessor can use depreciation shields more quickly than the lessee (e.g., a loss-making lessee).
  • GST applies on lease rentals; problems in your handout say "ignore GST".
  • Residual value: sale proceeds above book value are taxable (below: loss relief). In classroom problems, follow the question: "subject to tax" → tax it; otherwise take it as given.

Indian income tax uses the written-down-value (block of assets) method, and from 1 April 2026 the Income-tax Act, 2025 replaced the 1961 Act. Classroom problems (including all five handout problems) simplify to straight-line depreciation and a flat tax rate. Use the question's method; add a one-line note only if asked about practice.

4.4 Accounting: Ind AS 116 (lessee and lessor)

Ind AS 116 (Leases), effective for accounting periods beginning on or after 1 April 2019 in India (converged with IFRS 16), requires a lessee to recognise, for almost all leases, a right-of-use (ROU) asset and a lease liability measured at the present value of lease payments; the lessee then charges depreciation on the ROU asset and interest on the liability. Lessors continue to classify leases as finance or operating.

Lessee accounting (single model)

  1. Initial: Lease liability = PV of unpaid lease payments discounted at the rate implicit in the lease (or the lessee's incremental borrowing rate). ROU asset = lease liability + initial direct costs + prepaid rentals + restoration costs − incentives.
  2. Subsequent: interest on the liability (effective interest method) reduces with time; ROU asset depreciated (usually straight line over the lease term).
  3. Exemptions: short-term leases (12 months or less) and leases of low-value assets may be expensed on a straight-line basis.
  4. Effects: assets and debt rise (higher debt-equity); EBITDA rises (rent replaced by depreciation + interest); expense is front-loaded; operating cash flow rises and financing outflow rises (principal repayment).

Lessor accounting

  • Finance lease (substantially all risks and rewards transferred): derecognise the asset, recognise a lease receivable equal to the net investment; recognise finance income over the term.
  • Operating lease: keep the asset and depreciate it; recognise rental income on a straight-line basis.
  • Indicators of a finance lease: ownership transfers at end; bargain purchase option; term is the major part of economic life; PV of payments ≈ substantially all of fair value; specialised asset.

4.5 Evaluation from the lessee's point of view: lease vs buy

The lease vs buy decision compares the present value of the after-tax cash outflows of leasing with the present value of the after-tax cash outflows of buying (owning); the alternative with the lower PV of cost is chosen. Equivalently, Net Advantage of Leasing (NAL) = PV cost of buying − PV cost of leasing; lease if NAL > 0.

PV cost of leasing = Σ Lease rentalt × (1 − T) × PVFt   (rentals in advance: first one at t = 0)

4.6 Evaluation from the lessor's point of view

The lessor treats the lease as an investment: the outflow is the asset cost; the inflows are the after-tax lease rentals, the depreciation tax shields and the after-tax residual value. The lease is acceptable if its NPV at the lessor's cost of capital is positive. The break-even lease rental is the rental at which NPV = 0 (the minimum the lessor should charge).

NPVlessor = −Cost + Σ [R(1 − T) + Dep × T] × PVFt + After-tax RV × PVFn (− initial direct costs)
Break-even rental R* = [ (Cost − PV of RV) / AF − Dep × T ] / (1 − T)

4.7 Hire purchase: meaning and features

A hire purchase is "rent now, own later": you take the tractor home today after a down payment, pay instalments that include interest, and the tractor becomes yours only when the last instalment is paid. Miss instalments and the financier can take it back.

Hire purchase (HP) is an agreement under which the owner (hire vendor/financier) delivers goods to the hirer, who pays for them in periodic instalments (including an interest or hire charge); the hirer has an option to purchase the goods on paying the last instalment, and ownership passes to the hirer only when all instalments are paid. Until then the hirer is a bailee and may return the goods.

Features

  1. Possession passes immediately; ownership passes on payment of the last instalment.
  2. Down payment is usually required; the balance plus interest is paid in instalments.
  3. Each instalment has two parts: principal (cash price) and interest (hire charges).
  4. The hirer may terminate by returning the goods; instalments already paid are treated as hire charges (not refunded).
  5. On default, the vendor can repossess the goods (subject to the agreement and fair-practice norms).
  6. Tax: the hirer claims depreciation on the full cash price and deducts the interest element; the vendor is taxed on the interest (finance) income.
  7. Used mainly for vehicles, tractors, construction equipment and consumer durables, typically through NBFCs such as Sundaram Finance and Shriram Finance.

4.8 HP interest: flat rate, effective rate and sum-of-digits

Flat-rate interest = Amount financed × flat rate × years    Instalment = (Amount financed + total interest) / number of instalments
Effective rate r: Amount financed = Σ Instalment / (1 + r)t   (the IRR); rule of thumb r ≈ 2 × n × F / (n + 1)
Sum-of-digits (Rule of 78) interest for period t = Total interest × (remaining periods including t) / (1 + 2 + … + n)

F = flat rate, n = number of instalments. The flat rate charges interest on the original amount even though the balance falls, so the effective (true) rate is nearly double.

4.12 RBI guidelines relevant to hire purchase

In India, hire purchase finance is provided mainly by NBFCs regulated by the Reserve Bank of India under the RBI Act, 1934, and by banks. The Hire-Purchase Act, 1972 was passed by Parliament but was never brought into force, so HP agreements rest on the Indian Contract Act and the terms of the agreement, with RBI regulation of the lenders. Key points (at the level expected in the exam):

  1. Registration and classification: NBFCs must be registered with the RBI; in 2019 the RBI merged Asset Finance Companies, Loan Companies and Investment Companies into one category, NBFC – Investment and Credit Company (NBFC-ICC). Since October 2022 NBFCs are regulated under a Scale-Based Regulation framework (base, middle, upper and top layers).
  2. Prudential norms: capital adequacy, income recognition, asset classification (NPA) and provisioning apply to HP and lease assets.
  3. Fair Practices Code: loan terms, the rate of interest and the approach to risk must be communicated clearly; interest should be disclosed as an annualised rate so the borrower knows the true cost (not just a flat rate).
  4. Repossession: must follow the terms of the contract, which must be transparent; the borrower must get notice, a chance to pay before repossession and before sale, and recovery agents must not harass or intimidate.
  5. Deposit acceptance: only specifically permitted NBFCs may accept public deposits, within limits and credit-rating conditions.
  6. KYC, grievance redressal and the RBI Ombudsman scheme apply to NBFC customers.

Regulation of NBFCs is updated frequently. In an answer, give the principles (registration, prudential norms, fair practices, transparent pricing, lawful repossession, grievance redressal) rather than specific numeric limits unless your class notes gave them.

4.13 Problems of hire purchase in India

  1. High effective cost — flat-rate quotations hide effective rates nearly double the stated rate.
  2. Weak legal framework — no operative HP statute; disputes rely on contract law and courts, which are slow.
  3. Repossession disputes — coercive recovery practices led to litigation and RBI/court intervention; legitimate repossession is also slow.
  4. Credit risk — lending to small transport operators, farmers and first-time borrowers with irregular incomes; NPAs rise in downturns (e.g., commercial vehicle slumps).
  5. Funding constraints for NBFCs — reliance on bank and market borrowing; the 2018 IL&FS default tightened liquidity for NBFCs.
  6. Competition from banks offering cheaper vehicle loans, squeezing HP margins.
  7. Tax and documentation complexity; GST on some components.
  8. Customer awareness — hirers often do not understand the total cost or their rights.

4.14 IndiGo and Air India: aircraft leasing in practice

IndiGo (InterGlobe Aviation) built India's largest airline on an asset-light model: it places very large orders with Airbus (which earns bulk discounts) and, on delivery, typically sells the aircraft to leasing companies and leases them back (sale and leaseback). The discount captured at purchase, rather than aircraft ownership, is part of its economics; operating leases keep the fleet young, fuel-efficient and flexible. Under Ind AS 116 these leases now appear as ROU assets and lease liabilities.

Air India, after its acquisition by the Tata group in 2022, announced in 2023 a very large order of about 470 aircraft from Airbus and Boeing, and also inducted leased aircraft to add capacity quickly while new deliveries were awaited — a mix of ownership (financed through debt/ECBs and sale-and-leaseback) and leasing.

Lessor risk: when Go First entered insolvency in 2023, the moratorium under the Insolvency and Bankruptcy Code initially prevented lessors from repossessing their aircraft, highlighting repossession risk in India. The government has also promoted aircraft leasing from GIFT City IFSC so that Indian airlines can lease from domestic entities.

Why airlines lease: huge asset cost, rapid technology change (fuel efficiency), need for fleet flexibility, cross-border tax efficiency of lessors, and conserving capital for operations — all points to write in a leasing-importance answer.

5.1 Corporate financial strategy and corporate value

A company creates value only when the return it earns on the money invested in it is higher than the return investors could earn elsewhere at similar risk (its cost of capital). Profit alone is not enough: a company earning 10% on capital that costs 12% reports a profit but destroys value.

Corporate value is the present value of the future free cash flows the firm is expected to generate, discounted at its cost of capital. Corporate financial strategy aims to maximise this value by making investment, financing and distribution decisions that earn returns above the cost of capital, sustain growth and manage risk. Value-based management (VBM) is the management approach that aligns the firm's strategy, planning, performance measurement and incentives with the creation of shareholder value.

Three levers of value

  1. Earn a spread — raise return on invested capital (ROIC) above WACC (operating efficiency, pricing power, asset turnover).
  2. Grow where the spread is positive — invest more in businesses with ROIC > WACC; growth with a negative spread destroys value.
  3. Reduce the cost of capital and extend the competitive-advantage period — optimal capital structure, lower risk, durable moats.

TCS and Infosys earn returns on capital far above their cost of capital with little debt and modest capital needs, which is why their market values are many times their book values (large MVA). Capital-intensive businesses such as steel must work harder to earn a positive spread through the cycle.

5.3 Return on Investment (ROI)

Return on Investment (ROI) measures the return generated by a company from the capital or investment employed in the business. It indicates how efficiently the company uses its invested resources to generate operating returns. (Course handout definition.)

ROI = (PAT ÷ Capital Invested) × 100    (course formula)

Significance (handout): measures efficiency of investment; helps evaluate operating performance; compares projects or business units; higher ROI = better use of capital; compare ROI with the cost of capital to judge adequacy.

Your handout uses PAT in the numerator. Many textbooks define ROI (return on capital employed, ROCE) as EBIT ÷ capital employed or NOPAT ÷ invested capital (ROIC), because PAT is after interest and so mixes financing with operating performance. In the exam use PAT ÷ capital invested as primary; if EBIT is given you may add "on an EBIT basis, ROCE = …" as a one-line note. Divisional ROI (Du Pont) = profit margin × investment turnover.

Worked example

PAT ₹12 crore; capital invested ₹80 crore; EBIT ₹18 crore; cost of capital 11%. ROI (course) = 12/80 = 15% > 11%, adequate. ROCE on EBIT = 18/80 = 22.5%. Limitation: a division with ROI 25% may reject a project earning 18% (above the 11% cost of capital) because it lowers the division's average ROI — a key reason EVA was developed.

5.4 Return on Equity (ROE) and the DuPont analysis

Return on Equity (ROE) measures the profit earned by a company in relation to shareholders' equity; it shows the return generated for the owners. (Course handout.)

ROE = (Profit After Tax ÷ Shareholders' Equity) × 100    Shareholders' Equity = Share Capital + Reserves and Surplus
DuPont (3-step): ROE = Net profit margin × Asset turnover × Equity multiplier = (PAT/Sales) × (Sales/Total assets) × (Total assets/Equity)
5-step: ROE = (PAT/PBT) × (PBT/EBIT) × (EBIT/Sales) × (Sales/Assets) × (Assets/Equity) = tax burden × interest burden × operating margin × turnover × leverage

Significance (handout): profitability from shareholders' perspective; effectiveness in using shareholders' funds; comparison across years; influenced by operating performance and financial leverage.

5.5 Cash flow (CF) as a value driver

Value is the present value of cash, not profit. Key cash-flow measures: cash flow from operations (CFO); free cash flow to the firm (FCFF) = CFO − capex (approximately); free cash flow to equity (FCFE) = FCFF − interest(1 − T) + net borrowing; cash conversion = CFO / EBITDA or CFO / PAT. Drivers: revenue growth, margins, working-capital efficiency (operating cycle), capex intensity, tax.

Profits can be inflated by accruals (aggressive revenue recognition, capitalising costs); cash flows are harder to manipulate. A firm with high profit but weak CFO is often building up receivables or inventory — a warning sign.

5.6 Shareholder Value Added (SVA) and the Rappaport model

Shareholder Value Added (SVA) measures the increase in wealth attributable to shareholders over a period; it can consider both increase in share value and dividends. (Course handout.) In Rappaport's framework, SVA is the value created by a strategy: the present value of the future free cash flows (plus residual value) generated by the strategy minus the pre-strategy value of the business.

Course (simplified): SVA = (Closing Market Value − Opening Market Value) + Dividends
Rappaport: Shareholder value = Corporate value − Debt; Corporate value = PV of FCF during the forecast period + PV of residual value + marketable securities
SVA (strategy) = Post-strategy value − Pre-strategy value; pre-strategy value = current NOPAT / k
FCFt = Salest × Operating margin × (1 − cash tax rate) − (incremental fixed + working capital investment)
Threshold margin = (incremental investment rate × k) / [(1 + k)(1 − T)] — the minimum margin on incremental sales that keeps SVA ≥ 0

Rappaport's seven value drivers

  1. Sales growth rate
  2. Operating profit margin
  3. Cash tax rate
  4. Incremental fixed-capital investment (as % of incremental sales)
  5. Incremental working-capital investment (as % of incremental sales)
  6. Planning period / value growth duration (competitive-advantage period)
  7. Cost of capital

The first five drive operating cash flows; the sixth sets how long value is created; the seventh converts cash flows into value.

Key steps in firm forecasting analysis for SVA (and EVA)

  1. Analyse historical performance (growth, margins, capital intensity, tax, ROIC) — the base for forecasts.
  2. Choose the planning horizon (value growth duration: how long can the firm earn above its cost of capital?).
  3. Forecast the value drivers for each year (sales growth, margin, tax, fixed and working-capital investment rates).
  4. Compute NOPAT and free cash flow (for SVA) or NOPAT and capital charge (for EVA) for each year.
  5. Estimate the cost of capital (WACC via CAPM and after-tax cost of debt).
  6. Compute residual/continuing value at the end of the horizon (perpetuity of NOPAT or EVA).
  7. Discount to present value; compute corporate value, shareholder value and SVA (or MVA as PV of EVAs).
  8. Test sensitivity of value to each driver and to scenarios; identify the drivers that matter most.
  9. Translate into targets and incentives (VBM).

5.7 Economic Value Added (EVA)

Your shop made ₹12 lakh profit after tax, but you had ₹90 lakh of your own and borrowed money tied up in it, and that money could have earned ₹9 lakh elsewhere at similar risk. Your true (economic) profit is only ₹3 lakh. That ₹3 lakh is EVA.

Economic Value Added (EVA) measures the economic profit created by a company after deducting the cost of all capital employed, including debt and equity. (Course handout.) The concept was popularised (and trademarked) by Stern Stewart & Co.

EVA = NOPAT − (Invested Capital × WACC)    (course formula)
Equivalently EVA = (ROIC − WACC) × Invested Capital,   ROIC = NOPAT / Invested Capital
NOPAT = EBIT × (1 − T)    WACC = weke + wdkd(1 − T)    Invested capital = equity + debt (= net operating assets)

Significance (handout): EVA > 0 economic value created; EVA = 0 required return exactly earned; EVA < 0 value destroyed; shows whether the company earns more than its cost of capital; focuses management on value creation rather than accounting profit.

Common EVA accounting adjustments (Stern Stewart)

Adjustments convert accounting figures into economic ones: capitalise R&D and advertising/brand-building (add back the expense, amortise over useful life); add back increases in provisions (bad-debt, warranty) and deferred tax; treat operating leases as debt (now largely done by Ind AS 116); add back goodwill amortisation/impairment; remove non-operating assets and income; use cash taxes rather than book tax. Firms use only the few that matter for them.

Worked example 1 — basic EVA (capital = equity + debt)

Equity ₹60 crore (ke 12%), debt ₹30 crore (kd 8%, tax 25%), NOPAT ₹12 crore.

  1. After-tax kd = 8% × 0.75 = 6%; WACC = (60 × 12% + 30 × 6%)/90 = (7.2 + 1.8)/90 = 10%.
  2. Capital charge = 90 × 10% = ₹9 crore; EVA = 12 − 9 = ₹3 crore (value created).
  3. In the handout ABC problem a separate capital employed of ₹70 crore is given — use that as invested capital there: EVA = 12 − 7 = ₹5 crore (Problem 6).

  1. Unadjusted: NOPAT = 120 × 0.75 = 90; charge = 600 × 12% = 72; EVA = 18.
  2. Adjusted NOPAT = (120 + 24 − 8 + 4) × 0.75 = 140 × 0.75 = 105.

  4. Adjusted EVA = 105 − 79.2 = ₹25.8 crore: treating R&D as an investment rather than an expense shows more value creation.

5.8 Market Value Added (MVA)

5.9 Cash Value Added (CVA)

Cash Value Added (CVA) measures the cash value generated by a business after considering the cash required to provide an adequate return on the capital invested. (Course handout.) The model was developed by Fredrik Weissenrieder, where CVA = operating cash flow (OCF) − operating cash flow demand (OCFD), and OCFD is the constant (annuity) cash flow needed to recover the strategic investment and earn the cost of capital on it.

Course (simplified): CVA = Operating Cash Flow − Cash Capital Charge;   Cash Capital Charge = Invested Capital × Required Rate of Return
Weissenrieder: CVA = OCF − OCFD;   OCFD = Strategic investment / Annuity factor (WACC, life) (a cash charge covering both economic depreciation and return)
When economic depreciation is given separately: CVA = OCF − Economic depreciation − (Capital × WACC)
CVA index = OCF / OCFD (> 1 means value created)

Significance (handout): cash-based value creation; tests whether operating cash flows compensate capital providers; less dependent on accounting profit; useful for sustainable performance.

5.10 Cash Flow Return on Investment (CFROI)

CFROI (developed by HOLT Value Associates and associated with the Boston Consulting Group) is the internal rate of return that equates the firm's inflation-adjusted gross cash flows over the economic life of its assets (plus the release of non-depreciating assets such as land and working capital at the end) with its inflation-adjusted gross investment. It is compared with the (real) cost of capital: CFROI > cost of capital means value is created.

Gross investment = Σ Gross cash flow / (1 + CFROI)t + Non-depreciating assets / (1 + CFROI)n

Gross investment = gross (undepreciated) plant, adjusted for inflation, + non-depreciating assets; gross cash flow = NOPAT + depreciation (+ other non-cash items); n = average economic life of depreciating assets.

Worked example (₹ crore)

Gross investment 1,000 (800 depreciating assets + 200 working capital and land); gross cash flow 150 a year for a 10-year asset life; the 200 of non-depreciating assets is released at the end.

Solve 1,000 = 150 × AF(r, 10) + 200/(1 + r)10 → CFROI ≈ 9.97%. If the real cost of capital is 8%, the business earns a positive spread of about 2 percentage points. (Ignoring the release of 200, the rate would be only 8.14% — forgetting non-depreciating assets understates CFROI.)

Merits: cash-based, inflation-adjusted, comparable across firms, countries and time; less distorted by asset age than ROI. Limits: complex inputs (asset life, inflation adjustment); an IRR concept, so it inherits IRR's weaknesses.

One-line difference for exams: EVA is a rupee measure based on adjusted accounting profit; CFROI is a percentage measure based on gross cash flows and gross investment, compared with a real cost of capital.

5.11 SFROI

SFROI appears in your syllabus alongside EVA, SVA, CVA and CFROI, but it is not defined in your handouts and it is not a standard, widely published metric in finance literature (unlike EVA, CFROI or CVA). In this guide it is taught with a clearly labelled working definition. If your class notes defined it differently, use your notes.

Working definition used here: SFROI (Shareholders' Funds Return on Investment) is the return earned on the shareholders' funds invested in the business — profit attributable to equity shareholders divided by shareholders' funds (share capital + reserves), assessed against the cost of equity. Value is created for shareholders when SFROI exceeds the cost of equity.

SFROI = Profit attributable to equity shareholders / Shareholders' funds × 100    Spread = SFROI − ke

Under this definition it is conceptually the same as ROE used as a strategy-evaluation hurdle: strategies are judged by whether they raise the equity spread (SFROI − ke) without excessive leverage.

Worked example

ABC handout data: PAT ₹10 crore; shareholders' funds ₹60 crore; ke 12%. SFROI = 16.67%; equity spread = 4.67 percentage points; equity-level value added = 60 × 4.67% = ₹2.8 crore (= 10 − 60 × 12%).

5.13 VBM implementation in SFM

Value-based management is a management philosophy and system in which the objective of maximising shareholder value guides strategy, resource allocation, performance measurement and compensation throughout the organisation.

Steps to implement VBM

  1. Commit to value as the governing objective (board and top management).
  2. Choose the value metric (EVA, SVA, CVA, CFROI) suited to the business and understandable to managers.
  3. Identify value drivers at each level (group, business unit, operations) — build a value-driver tree.
  4. Set targets for drivers and metrics (e.g., EVA improvement, ROIC above WACC).
  5. Integrate into planning and capital allocation — evaluate strategies by value created; allocate capital to units with positive spreads.
  6. Align incentives — link bonuses to EVA improvement (bonus banks to encourage long-term thinking).
  7. Communicate and train — managers must understand how their decisions move value drivers.
  8. Monitor and review — performance reports on value metrics, post-audits, feedback into the strategic plan.

Principles of strategy evaluation based on VBM models

  • A strategy is acceptable only if it creates value: PV of EVAs > 0 / SVA > 0 / PV of CVAs > 0 / CFROI > real cost of capital.
  • Evaluate over the full competitive-advantage period, not one year.
  • Compare with the "do nothing" (pre-strategy) value.
  • Test sensitivity to key drivers; prefer strategies robust across scenarios.
  • Value = cash, time and risk: judge by cash flows discounted at a risk-appropriate rate.

Pitfalls of VBM: metric complexity; short-termism if bonuses depend on one-year EVA; manipulation by cutting valuable long-term spending (R&D, training); ignoring stakeholders and ESG (which can destroy long-term value).

Reliance and HUL (course plan examples): HUL generates high returns on a small capital base (strong brands, negative working capital) — high ROIC, high EVA spread, very high MVA. Reliance deploys very large capital in new businesses; its value creation depends on those investments eventually earning above their cost of capital — an SVA/EVA forecasting question rather than a current-year one.

Theory points from your notes

  • Corporate financial strategy: long-term financial decisions and policies.
  • Corporate value (economic value): the increase in the value of the company, seen through a rise in market value and in asset value.
  • Perspectives of corporate value: (1) accounting perspective (asset-based); (2) business valuation — income-based, market-based and asset-based approaches. Business value (asset approach) = fair value of assets − liabilities. Income-based approach = DCF and capitalisation of earnings.
  • Value drivers used to analyse performance: EVA, MVA, SVA, CVA, ROI, ROE. Business, market and financial value drivers together show not only profitability but stability — whether the company meets its financial commitments, increases shareholders' wealth and has enough funds for operations.
  • EVA — a financial performance metric measuring the difference between net operating profit after tax and the cost of capital; it shows how well the company uses its capital to generate returns. Cost of capital = capital employed × WACC. Positive EVA = good financial performance.
  • MVA — measures value added to shareholders' wealth: market value of equity − capital invested (book value of shares).
  • SVA — closing market value − opening market value + dividends.
  • CVA — operating cash flow − cash capital charges.
  • Dividend theories: relevance theories (dividend decision affects share price) — Walter and Gordon; irrelevance theory (no effect on share price) — Modigliani and Miller.

Short-answer questions (2–3 marks) — 18

1. Define strategic financial management.

Application of financial management techniques to the firm's long-term strategic objectives — investment, financing, dividend, working-capital and risk strategies — to maximise long-run value.

2. Distinguish objectives and goals.

Objectives are broad, long-term, often qualitative ends (maximise wealth); goals are specific, measurable, time-bound targets (ROE 18% by FY2029).

3. What is a sustainability-linked bond?

A bond whose coupon changes depending on whether the issuer meets pre-set ESG targets; unlike a green bond, proceeds need not go to specific green projects.

4. What is the coefficient of variation of NPV and why is it used?

CV = σNPV/ENPV; it measures risk per rupee of expected NPV and allows comparison of projects of different sizes.

5. What is a break-even (critical) value in sensitivity analysis?

The value of an input at which NPV becomes zero; the % change from the base value shows how sensitive the project is to that input.

6. State two assumptions of Walter's model.

All investment is financed by retained earnings; r and k remain constant (also: constant E and D, infinite life).

7. What is a stable dividend policy?

Paying a constant or steadily rising dividend per share, changing it only when a new level is sustainable; it provides predictable income and a positive signal.

8. What is the clientele effect?

Investors choose firms whose payout suits their needs (income vs growth); a change in policy may shift the investor base.

9. What is permanent working capital?

The minimum level of current assets needed at all times to run operations; it should be financed with long-term funds.

10. What is commercial paper?

An unsecured money-market instrument issued at a discount by highly rated companies for 7 days to 1 year, regulated by the RBI.

11. Cost of forgoing "2/10 net 30"?

(2/98) × (360/20) = 36.7% a year — so taking the discount is usually worthwhile.

12. What is safety stock?

Buffer inventory held to guard against demand or lead-time variability: (maximum usage − average usage) × lead time, or Z × σ × √lead time.

13. What is a leveraged lease?

A lease in which the lessor finances most of the asset cost with non-recourse debt from a third-party lender secured on the asset and rentals.

14. What is a right-of-use asset?

Under Ind AS 116, the lessee's asset representing its right to use the leased asset over the lease term, initially measured at the lease liability plus direct costs and prepayments.

15. Why is the flat rate misleading in HP?

It charges interest on the original balance throughout, although the balance declines; the effective rate is nearly double (≈ 2nF/(n + 1)).

16. Define NOPAT.

Net operating profit after tax = EBIT × (1 − tax rate); operating profit available to all capital providers, before financing costs.

17. What is CFROI?

The IRR of a firm's inflation-adjusted gross cash flows (plus release of non-depreciating assets) on its gross investment, compared with the real cost of capital.

18. What is value-based management?

A management system in which shareholder-value creation guides strategy, capital allocation, performance measurement and pay, using metrics such as EVA or SVA.

Common mistakes and examiner traps

  1. Discounting certainty-equivalent cash flows at RADR or WACC (risk counted twice). CE → rf.
  2. Using company WACC for a project riskier than average — accepts value-destroying projects (see the Tata Steel RADR example).
  3. Discounting variances by (1 + r)t instead of (1 + r)2t in the independent case, or adding SDs when flows are independent.
  4. Reporting the Z-table value instead of 1 − value (or the reverse) for P(NPV < 0).
  5. Not pruning branches at decision nodes, or forgetting to subtract the decision's cost, in decision trees.
  6. Walter: writing (r/k)D instead of (r/k)(E − D); using percentages instead of decimals.
  7. Gordon: using it when k ≤ br; mixing D0 and D1.
  8. MM: issuing new shares at P0 instead of P1; forgetting that m changes with the dividend.
  9. Operating cycle: dividing WIP or FG by sales instead of cost of production / COGS; mixing 360 and 365.
  10. Miller-Orr: annual interest rate with daily variance; SD not squared.
  11. Credit policy: investment in receivables at sales value when the question says "cost" (or not stating the basis).
  12. Lease vs buy: giving the lessee a depreciation shield under leasing; forgetting depreciation shields under buying/HP.
  13. HP: deducting the whole instalment for tax instead of only interest; forgetting the year-5 depreciation shield after the last instalment.
  14. Residual value: ignoring it, or not taxing it when the question says it is taxable (or taxing it when book value equals sale price).
  15. Putting the down payment in year 1 instead of year 0; treating year-end rentals as advance payments (or the reverse).
  16. EVA: using PAT without saying so, or using equity only as capital when WACC includes debt.
  17. MVA: comparing market value of equity with total capital (mismatch) — equity with equity, firm with firm.
  18. No decision/interpretation line under a numerical — that line carries marks.

ESE

30 marks, 1 hour. A: 2 of 3 × 5 (12 min each). B: 20-mark case (28 min): issue → assumptions → computation → interpretation → recommendation.

Unit I — strategy

SFM = long-term, value, whole-firm, risk. Hierarchy: mission → objectives → goals → strategy → policy → tactics. Planning: objectives → environment → resources → options → evaluate/choose → financial plan → implement/control → feedback. Interface: funds need, risk balance, maturity match, WACC hurdle, control, flexibility, life cycle. Sustainable: g = ROE × b; green/SLB bonds; BRSR; Infosys, Tata Steel, ITC.

Unit I — risk

CE: αCF at rf. RADR: rf + premium or CAPM. ENPV = Σ E(CF)PVF − I. σ indep. √Σσ²/(1+r)2t; correlated Σσ/(1+r)t. CV = σ/ENPV. Z = (X − ENPV)/σ. Tree: average at ○, max at □, prune ∥. Sensitivity: NPV ÷ PV affected. Scenario: 3 states. Monte Carlo: model → distributions → RNs → repeat → analyse. Options: expand, abandon, delay, switch.

Unit II

Walter P = [D + (r/k)(E − D)]/k: r>k 0%; r=k any; r<k 100%. Gordon P = E(1−b)/(k−br), k > br. MM: P1 = P0(1+k) − D1; m = [I − (E − nD)]/P1; nP0 = [(n+m)P1 − I + E]/(1+k). Determinants: earnings, growth, liquidity, law, covenants, tax, control, signalling.

Unit III

NOC = RM + WIP + FG + Dr − Cr (days). Sources: trade credit, cash credit, OD, bill discounting, CP, factoring, deposits, ICDs. Approaches: matching / conservative / aggressive. Baumol √(2TF/i). M-O spread 3(¾Fσ²/i)1/3, RP L + S/3, UL L + S. Credit: contribution − bad debts − collection − (sales × VC% × days/360 × k). EOQ √(2AO/C). ROL = max use × max LT. WC out at start, back at end.

Unit IV

Lease: rental(1−T) only. Buy: cost − dep·T + maint(1−T) − RVAT. HP: down + instalment − interest·T − dep·T. Lower PV wins; NAL = buy − lease. Lessor NPV: −cost + [R(1−T) + dep·T] + RV. Ind AS 116: ROU + liability; exemptions ≤12 months, low value. HP ownership at end; flat ≈ half effective; sum-of-digits.

Unit V

ROI = PAT/capital invested. ROE = PAT/(SC + R&S) = margin × turnover × multiplier. EVA = NOPAT − IC × WACC. MVA = MV − BV equity = PV EVAs. SVA = ΔMV + dividends (Rappaport: 7 drivers). CVA = OCF − IC × k (OCF − OCFD). CFROI = IRR on gross investment. Value created when return > cost of capital.
