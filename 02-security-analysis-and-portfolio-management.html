Unit 1Introduction to Equity Investments

Syllabus: meaning and objectives of equity investment; savings and wealth creation; investment, speculation, gambling and arbitrage; systematic and unsystematic risk (market, business, financial, liquidity); risk–return analysis for a single security (HPR, total return, SD, variance, correlation, covariance); regulatory framework (SEBI, stock exchanges); profile of Indian equity investors and factors influencing equity investment decisions. 8 teaching hours.

1.1 Investment and equity investment

You give up spending ₹1 lakh today because you expect to have more than ₹1 lakh later. The "more" can come as regular income (interest, dividends) or as a rise in the value of what you bought (capital appreciation). That sacrifice-for-future-reward is investment.

Investment is the employment of funds on assets to earn income or capital appreciation. The individual who makes an investment is known as the investor.

Economic sense: investment is the net addition made to the capital stock of the country (factories, machines, roads). Financial sense: investment is allocating money to assets with a view to gain profit over a period of time.

Equity investment involves purchasing partial ownership stakes in a public or private company, usually through stocks or equity mutual funds. As a shareholder you are entitled to a portion of the company's assets and profits, with returns from capital appreciation and dividends.

Link between the two senses (from your slides): an individual's savings flow into the capital market as financial investment (buying shares in an IPO), and the company uses that money for economic investment (building a plant). So financial investment is the channel; economic investment is the destination.

Features (characteristics) of an investment

  1. Return — the reward: income + capital gain.
  2. Risk — the variability of that return; higher expected return normally requires accepting higher risk.
  3. Safety — security of the principal (government securities are safest in India).
  4. Liquidity — how quickly an asset can be converted to cash without loss of value (listed large-cap shares are highly liquid; real estate is not).
  5. Marketability / transferability — ease of transfer to another person.
  6. Time horizon — investment is long-term; speculation is short-term.
  7. Tax shelter — some avenues (PPF, ELSS under the old tax regime) give tax benefits.

Importance of savings and equity investment in wealth creation

Role of savings (slide): the base layer.

  • Emergency fund — 6 months of expenses kept liquid.
  • Capital preservation — money that must not fall in value.
  • Building a corpus — the pool that is later invested.

Role of equity — the wealth generator (slide):

  • Beating inflation — real (inflation-adjusted) growth.
  • Power of compounding — returns earn returns.
  • Wealth creation and dividends — ownership of growing businesses.

Compounding illustration. ₹1,00,000 kept for 20 years at 6% p.a. grows to 1,00,000 × 1.06²⁰ = ₹3,20,714. At 12% p.a. it grows to 1,00,000 × 1.12²⁰ = ₹9,64,629. Doubling the rate roughly triples the final amount — this is why the long-run return gap between deposits and equity matters so much.

India's household savings have traditionally been held in bank deposits, gold and real estate. The growth of demat accounts after 2020 and of monthly SIP (systematic investment plan) inflows into mutual funds, tracked by AMFI, shows savings shifting towards equity — the "financialisation of savings".

Savings alone preserve money; investment makes it grow. Without equity exposure, long-term goals are eroded by inflation (purchasing-power risk).

1.2 Investment vs speculation vs gambling vs arbitrage

Investor: buys a business to own it for years. Speculator: bets on a short-term price move using analysis, and accepts high risk. Gambler: bets on chance with no analysis. Arbitrageur: sees the same thing priced differently in two places and buys-low/sells-high simultaneously — almost no risk.

Speculation means taking investment risks with the anticipation of acquiring short-term gain; it involves buying and selling in order to profit from price fluctuations. The person is a speculator. Examples: real estate, commodities (futures contracts), shares (short selling).

Gambling is staking money on an uncertain event whose outcome depends mainly on chance, with no underlying economic activity and usually a negative expected value.

Arbitrage is a strategy that exploits temporary price differences for the same asset in different markets — buying where it is cheaper and simultaneously selling where it is dearer — so that prices eventually converge.

Arbitrage in India: many large companies (e.g. Reliance Industries, HDFC Bank) are listed on both NSE and BSE. If a share quotes ₹1,500.00 on NSE and ₹1,501.50 on BSE at the same moment, an arbitrageur buys on NSE and sells on BSE, locking in ₹1.50 per share before costs. "Cash-futures arbitrage" (buy in cash market, sell futures) is the strategy behind arbitrage mutual funds regulated by SEBI.

Speculation: intraday trading and index option buying on NSE. SEBI studies of the F&O segment have found that a large majority of individual traders lost money — the reason SEBI tightened derivative rules for retail participants.

Your slide calls arbitrage a "trading strategy". In strict finance theory, pure arbitrage is riskless and needs no capital (simultaneous buy and sell). In practice (and in Indian markets) arbitrage carries small execution, settlement and cost risk. Both views are correct in their context; in the exam, write "near-riskless".

The exam frequently asks you to distinguish these activities (General Question 1). Speculation is legitimate and useful to markets (liquidity and price discovery); gambling is not.

1. A person buys Nifty call options expecting a rise over the next week, using a technical breakout signal. Classify.

Speculation — short horizon, high calculated risk, based on market information.

2. Why does arbitrage make markets more efficient?

Buying in the cheap market raises its price; selling in the dear market lowers its price; the gap closes, so the same asset trades at one price (law of one price).

3. Give two differences between speculation and gambling.

(i) Speculation uses information and analysis; gambling relies on chance. (ii) Speculation provides liquidity and price discovery to markets; gambling has no economic function.

1.4 Risk in equity investment

Risk is expressed in terms of the variability of return — the chance that the actual return differs from the expected return. Total risk = systematic risk + unsystematic risk.

Systematic risk

Systematic risk refers to that portion of variation in return caused by factors that affect the prices of all securities. Its effect causes the prices of all individual securities to move in the same direction. It cannot be avoided and cannot be eliminated by diversification, because every share and bond is influenced by the general market trend.

Unsystematic risk is unique and specific to a particular company or industry. Causes: managerial inefficiency, changes in consumer preferences, availability of raw materials, labour problems. It can be reduced — almost eliminated — by diversification. Two main types: business risk and financial risk.

1. Which risk does financial leverage create, and where in the income statement does it act?

Financial risk; it acts between EBIT and EBT through fixed interest payments.

2. Can a portfolio of 50 Nifty stocks remove inflation risk?

No. Purchasing-power risk is systematic and affects all securities; diversification only removes unsystematic risk.

3. A strike at one cement plant — which risk?

Internal business risk (unsystematic).

1.5 Return on a single security: HPR and total return

If you buy a share for ₹100, receive ₹4 dividend, and sell for ₹110, you earned ₹14 on ₹100 — a 14% return for the period you held it. That is the holding period return.

Holding period return (HPR) is the total return earned on an investment over the period it was held, expressed as a percentage of the amount invested. Total return = income return (dividend yield) + capital gain/loss yield.

HPR = [ D + (P1 − P0) ] ÷ P0 × 100
Total return = D/P0 (dividend yield) + (P1 − P0)/P0 (capital-gain yield)
Annualised (compound) return = (1 + HPR)1/n − 1    n = years held

P0 = purchase price, P1 = selling (or current) price, D = dividends received during the holding period, n = holding period in years. Use HPR for any single holding; annualise when comparing holdings of different lengths.

Example 1 (basic). Bought at ₹250, dividend ₹5, sold at ₹280 after one year.

  1. Capital gain = 280 − 250 = ₹30.
  2. Total gain = 30 + 5 = ₹35.
  3. HPR = 35 ÷ 250 = 0.14 = 14% (dividend yield 2% + capital-gain yield 12%).

Example 2 (multi-year, annualise). Bought at ₹400, total dividends ₹24 over 3 years, sold at ₹520.

  1. HPR = (520 − 400 + 24) ÷ 400 = 144 ÷ 400 = 36%.
  2. Simple annual average = 36% ÷ 3 = 12% (ignores compounding).
  3. Compound annual return = 1.361/3 − 1 = 1.10793 − 1 = 10.79% p.a.

The compound figure is lower because each year's return is earned on a growing base.

Example 3 (reverse — find the selling price). You buy at ₹150 and expect a ₹6 dividend. What selling price gives an HPR of 20%?

  1. Required total ending value = 150 × 1.20 = ₹180.
  2. Selling price = 180 − 6 = ₹174.
  3. Check: (174 − 150 + 6) ÷ 150 = 30 ÷ 150 = 20%. ✔

Your course problems on HPR (₹50→₹60 with ₹3 dividend = 26%; ₹20,000→₹26,000 with ₹2,000 dividends) are solved in Course material — HPR.

Arithmetic mean vs geometric mean; real vs nominal return

Arithmetic mean (AM) = ΣRt ÷ n
Geometric mean (GM) = [ (1+R1)(1+R2)…(1+Rn) ]1/n − 1
Fisher: (1 + nominal) = (1 + real)(1 + inflation)  ⇒  real = (1+nominal)/(1+inflation) − 1 ≈ nominal − inflation

Use AM as the best estimate of a single future year's return; use GM for the actual compound growth achieved over several years. GM ≤ AM always (equal only if all returns are identical).

Example 4. Annual returns: +20%, −10%, +15%, +5%.

  1. AM = (20 − 10 + 15 + 5) ÷ 4 = 30 ÷ 4 = 7.50%.
  2. Growth factor = 1.20 × 0.90 × 1.15 × 1.05 = 1.3041.
  3. GM = 1.30411/4 − 1 = 6.86%.

1.7 Variance and standard deviation from historical data

Variance is the average of squared deviations of returns from their mean; standard deviation is its square root and measures total risk in the same units as return (%). A higher SD means returns are more dispersed and the security is riskier.

σ² = Σ (Rt − R̄)² ÷ n  (population)   or   s² = Σ (Rt − R̄)² ÷ (n − 1)  (sample)

R̄ = arithmetic mean of the n observed returns. Use (n − 1) when the data are a sample used to estimate the population (statistics convention; Excel STDEV.S); use n when the data are the whole population or when your textbook does so (Excel STDEV.P).

Many Indian SAPM textbooks divide by n for historical returns; statistics and Excel's default STDEV use n − 1. 1.8 Covariance and correlation

Covariance asks: when stock A is above its average, is stock B usually above its average too? Correlation is the same idea squeezed onto a −1 to +1 scale so you can read its strength directly.

Covariance measures the extent to which the returns of two securities move together (interactive risk). It is positive when returns move in the same direction, zero when they are independent, and negative when they move in opposite directions.

Correlation coefficient (r or ρ) is a standardised measure of the nature and extent of the relationship between two returns (e.g. a stock and the market index), ranging from −1 (perfect negative) to +1 (perfect positive).

1.9 Beta and alpha

Beta (β) is a measure of a stock's volatility or systematic risk compared with the overall market. It indicates how much a stock's price is likely to move in relation to market movements. β = 1: moves with the market; β > 1: more volatile (aggressive); β < 1: less volatile (defensive); β < 0: moves opposite to the market.

Alpha (α) measures the excess return of an investment compared with a benchmark after adjusting for its risk. Positive alpha = outperformance (manager skill); negative alpha = underperformance.

βi = Cov(Ri, Rm) ÷ σm²   =   rim × σi ÷ σm
Regression (characteristic line): Ri = α + β Rm + e
β = [nΣXY − ΣXΣY] ÷ [nΣX² − (ΣX)²]     α = Ȳ − βX̄   (X = Rm, Y = Ri)

Use the covariance form when you have σ's and r; use the regression form when you have paired return data. Interpretation: if the market rises 10%, a stock with β = 1.5 is expected to rise 15%.

1.12 Profile of Indian equity investors and factors influencing equity investment decisions

Two people can look at the same share of HDFC Bank and reach opposite decisions. A 24-year-old with a salary, no dependants and a 30-year horizon can ride out a 30% fall. A 62-year-old living on a pension may not be able to. Nothing about the share changed; the investor changed. Investor profiling is the step that matches the security to the person. The same idea explains why Indian households, who have traditionally preferred gold, property and bank deposits, have been moving into equity through SIPs and demat accounts over the last decade.

An investor profile is a description of an investor's financial situation, objectives, time horizon, liquidity needs, knowledge and risk tolerance (willingness and ability to bear loss). It is used to decide the asset allocation and the kind of equity exposure that suits that investor. Factors influencing equity investment decisions are the economic, personal, psychological, social, tax and market-related influences that determine whether, how much and in what form an individual invests in equity.

SEBI-registered investment advisers must carry out risk profiling and recommend only suitable products, so profiling is a regulatory duty as well as good practice. In the exam, questions on "factors influencing investment decisions" and "profile of Indian investors" are straightforward 5- or 10-mark theory questions. Introduction. An equity investment decision depends on the investor as much as on the security. It results from personal, financial, economic, regulatory, social and psychological factors, which together form the investor's profile.

7. Regulatory and technological factors. SEBI investor protection, T+1 settlement, e-KYC, discount brokers and UPI SIPs have lowered the barriers to entry.

8. Social factors. Family advice, peers, social media and a cultural preference for gold and property.

9. Behavioural biases. Overconfidence, herding, loss aversion, anchoring and the disposition effect lead to poor timing and under-diversification.

Q (5 marks). Distinguish between investment and speculation.

Introduction. Investment is the employment of funds on assets to earn income or capital appreciation, while speculation means taking investment risks with the anticipation of short-term gain from price fluctuations.

  1. Time horizon: investment is long-term; speculation is short-term.
  2. Risk: investor takes moderate, calculated risk; speculator takes high risk.
  3. Return: investor expects moderate, consistent returns (dividends + appreciation); speculator expects high returns from price changes.
  4. Basis of decision: investor relies on fundamentals; speculator relies on market information, technical charts and tips.
  5. Funds: investor uses own funds; speculator often uses borrowed funds or leverage (futures, margin trading).

Example. Buying Infosys shares to hold for 10 years for dividends and growth is investment; buying Nifty call options expecting a rise after an RBI policy announcement is speculation.

Conclusion. The two differ mainly in horizon and risk attitude; both are legal and speculation adds liquidity, unlike gambling.

Q (10 marks). Explain systematic and unsystematic risk with their types. How can an investor manage each?

Introduction. Risk is the variability of return. Total risk = systematic + unsystematic risk. [Draw the classification tree from Section 1.4.]

A. Systematic risk — portion of variation caused by factors affecting all securities; non-diversifiable; measured by beta.

  1. Market risk — caused by alternating bull and bear forces (Jack Francis); tangible events (war, earthquake) and intangible events (market psychology). E.g. Covid-19 crash, March 2020.
  2. Interest-rate risk — variation caused by market interest rates; affects bonds most; caused by monetary policy, T-bill and G-sec rates. E.g. RBI repo hikes of 2022–23.
  3. Purchasing-power risk — loss of purchasing power from inflation (demand-pull, cost-push).

B. Unsystematic risk — unique to a firm/industry; diversifiable.

  1. Business risk — inability to maintain growth/stability of earnings; internal (operational efficiency) and external (environment imposed).
  2. Financial risk — from debt in the capital structure; acts between EBIT and EBT.
  3. Liquidity risk — inability to sell quickly at a fair price.

C. Management. Unsystematic: diversify across 20–30 stocks in different industries; analyse capital structure. Systematic: cannot be diversified away — manage through asset allocation (equity–debt–gold), choosing lower-beta stocks, hedging with index futures/options, inflation-linked assets, and laddering bond maturities.

Conclusion. Because markets reward only systematic risk (CAPM), a rational investor diversifies away unsystematic risk and chooses the level of systematic risk that matches his objectives.

2.1 Concept and importance of fundamental analysis

Before buying a house you check the city (is it growing?), the neighbourhood (is it in demand?), and the house itself (is it sound, fairly priced?). Fundamental analysis does the same for a share: economy → industry → company → fair value.

Fundamental analysis is the examination of various factors such as the earnings of the company, its growth rate and its risk exposure that affect the value of its shares. It consists of economic analysis, industry analysis and company analysis (the EIC framework), and aims to estimate the intrinsic value of a share and compare it with the market price.

Importance / objectives of fundamental analysis

  1. Estimates intrinsic value to identify under-valued and over-valued shares.
  2. Assesses the financial health and earning capacity of a company.
  3. Supports long-term investment decisions (buy-and-hold).
  4. Helps forecast future earnings, cash flows and dividends using historical trends, growth rates, industry dynamics and management strategy (slide: "Prediction about market behaviour").
  5. Identifies risks — business, financial, governance.
  6. Provides the margin of safety needed by value investors.

Limitations: time-consuming; depends on accuracy of published accounts (window dressing, frauds such as Satyam); forecasts can be wrong; market price may stay away from intrinsic value for long periods; qualitative factors are hard to quantify.

Monetary policy and fiscal policy

Monetary policy refers to the actions of a country's central bank to manage the money supply, interest rates and overall economic activity to achieve goals such as controlling inflation, stabilising the currency, achieving full employment and fostering sustainable growth. In India it is conducted by the RBI's Monetary Policy Committee (MPC). Fiscal policy is the government's use of taxation and public expenditure (the Union Budget) to influence the economy.

2.4 Company analysis

Company analysis examines the growth and financial strength of an individual company so that the present and future value of its shares can be known.

Qualitative factors

  • Competitive edge (moat): brand, patents, cost advantage, network effects
  • Market share and its trend
  • Quality and integrity of management; promoter track record
  • Corporate governance: independent directors, related-party transactions, auditor quality, promoter pledging
  • Business model and product portfolio
  • Technology, R&D, labour relations
  • ESG practices

Quantitative factors

  • Growth of sales (slide)
  • Stability of sales (slide)
  • Earnings growth; EPS trend
  • Profitability ratios (margins, ROE, ROCE)
  • Capital structure (debt–equity, interest coverage)
  • Liquidity and efficiency ratios
  • Cash flows; dividend record
  • Valuation ratios (P/E, P/B)

Two companies in the same industry can deliver very different returns: governance failures (Satyam, 2009) or excessive debt can destroy value even in a growing sector. Qualitative analysis catches what ratios miss — especially in uncertain periods (General Question 7).

Techniques of analysis (slide)

  1. Comparative financial statements — current year's figures placed alongside the previous year's, with absolute and % change.
  2. Trend analysis — growth or decline of sales and profit over several years, usually as % of a base year (the slide's "percentage change analysis" from 2006).
  3. Common-size statements — each income-statement item as % of net sales; each balance-sheet item as % of total assets. Allows comparison of firms of different sizes and with the industry.
  4. Fund-flow analysis — statement of sources and applications of funds (working capital basis).
  5. Cash-flow analysis — inflows and outflows of cash during the year.
  6. Ratio analysis — numerical relationship between two items.

Example 1 (common-size and trend). Sales rise from ₹800 crore (base year) to ₹1,000 crore; PAT rises from ₹56 crore to ₹80 crore.

  1. Common-size PAT: base year 56/800 = 7.0% of sales; current 80/1,000 = 8.0% of sales — margin improved.
  2. Trend (base = 100): sales 1,000/800 × 100 = 125; PAT 80/56 × 100 = 142.9. Profit grew faster than sales → operating leverage/efficiency gains.

2.7 DuPont analysis

DuPont analysis breaks ROE into its drivers so an analyst can see why ROE is high or low: profitability (net margin), efficiency (asset turnover) and leverage (equity multiplier).

3-step: ROE = (PAT/Sales) × (Sales/Total assets) × (Total assets/Equity) = NPM × ATO × EM
5-step: ROE = (PAT/PBT) × (PBT/EBIT) × (EBIT/Sales) × (Sales/TA) × (TA/Equity)
= tax burden × interest burden × operating margin × asset turnover × equity multiplier

Example 2 (Sahyadri).

  1. NPM = 12.5%; ATO = 1,200 ÷ 1,300 = 0.9231; EM = 1,300 ÷ 750 = 1.7333.
  2. ROE = 0.125 × 0.9231 × 1.7333 = 0.20 = 20% ✔ (matches PAT ÷ equity).
  3. 5-step: tax burden 150/200 = 0.75; interest burden 200/240 = 0.8333; operating margin 240/1,200 = 0.20; ATO 0.9231; EM 1.7333. Product = 0.75 × 0.8333 × 0.20 × 0.9231 × 1.7333 = 0.20. ✔

Example 3 (compare two firms). Firm P: NPM 5%, ATO 2.0, EM 2.5. Firm Q: NPM 15%, ATO 0.8, EM 1.25.

  1. ROEP = 0.05 × 2.0 × 2.5 = 25%; ROEQ = 0.15 × 0.8 × 1.25 = 15%.
  2. ROAP = 0.05 × 2.0 = 10%; ROAQ = 0.15 × 0.8 = 12%.
  3. Conclusion: P's higher ROE comes from leverage (EM 2.5), not better operations — Q genuinely earns more on its assets. P carries more financial risk.

2.9 Margin of safety

A bridge rated for 30 tonnes should carry only 10-tonne trucks. Similarly, a share you estimate is worth ₹250 should be bought only well below ₹250, so that even if your estimate is too optimistic you do not lose.

Margin of safety (investment sense — Benjamin Graham) is the difference between a share's intrinsic value and its market price, expressed as a percentage of intrinsic value. It is a cushion against errors in analysis and bad luck.

Margin of safety (cost-accounting / break-even sense) is the excess of actual sales over break-even sales; it shows how far sales can fall before the company makes a loss.

Investment MOS (%) = (Intrinsic value − Market price) ÷ Intrinsic value × 100
Break-even MOS (₹) = Actual sales − Break-even sales    MOS (%) = MOS ÷ Actual sales × 100
Break-even sales = Fixed costs ÷ P/V ratio   (P/V ratio = contribution ÷ sales)

Example 4 (investment MOS). Intrinsic value ₹250, market price ₹180.

  1. MOS = (250 − 180) ÷ 250 = 70 ÷ 250 = 28%.
  2. Decision: a value investor requiring at least 25–30% would buy.
  3. Reverse: to get a 35% MOS, maximum buying price = 250 × (1 − 0.35) = ₹162.50.

Example 5 (break-even MOS). Sales ₹1,200 crore, break-even sales ₹800 crore.

  1. MOS = 1,200 − 800 = ₹400 crore.
  2. MOS % = 400 ÷ 1,200 = 33.33% — sales can fall by a third before losses begin.

Your TechNova problem (slide 67) asks for MOS using actual vs break-even sales — the cost-accounting sense. The syllabus phrase "margin of safety concept" in fundamental analysis usually means Graham's intrinsic-value sense. Know both, and label which one you are computing.

Value investors in India often cite the margin-of-safety principle when buying quality companies during broad sell-offs (e.g. March 2020), when prices of fundamentally sound firms fell well below most estimates of intrinsic value.

Q (10 marks). Explain the EIC framework of fundamental analysis.

Introduction. Fundamental analysis examines factors such as earnings, growth and risk that determine the value of a share. It follows a top-down Economy–Industry–Company (EIC) approach. [Draw the funnel.]

1. Economy analysis. Studies macro factors — GDP, savings and investment, inflation, interest rates, Budget, tax structure, exchange rate, monsoon. Uses leading (monetary/fiscal policy, rainfall), coincident (GDP, IIP) and lagging (unemployment, CPI) indicators, and the business cycle. Expansionary RBI policy supports equity; tightening hurts rate-sensitive sectors.

2. Industry analysis. Classifies industries (growth, cyclical, defensive, cyclical-growth); locates the industry in its life cycle (pioneering, expansion, stagnation, decay); applies Porter's five forces; studies cost structure, competition, government policy.

3. Company analysis. Qualitative — management, competitive edge, market share, governance. Quantitative — sales growth and stability, ratio analysis (liquidity, leverage, efficiency, profitability, valuation), DuPont, cash-flow quality.

4. Valuation. Estimate intrinsic value (DDM, P/E) and compare with price; buy with a margin of safety.

Example. After the 2019 corporate tax cut (economy), an investor might favour consumer and capital-goods industries (industry) and choose the leader with high ROE and low debt (company).

Conclusion. EIC ensures a good company is bought in a supportive industry and economy at a sensible price.

Q (5 marks). What is the industry life cycle? How is it useful to an investor?

The industry life cycle describes the stages an industry passes through: (1) Pioneering — new technology, many entrants, low profits, high risk; (2) Expansion — weak firms exit, leaders grow rapidly — best stage to invest; (3) Stagnation/maturity — growth slows to GDP pace, high dividends — hold for income; (4) Decay — demand falls due to obsolescence — exit. [Sketch the S-curve.] Usefulness: tells the investor what growth rate to assume in valuation (high growth in expansion, stable growth in maturity), the risk level, and when to enter or exit. Example: renewable energy in India is in expansion; landline telephony is in decay.

Q (5 marks). Explain the concept of margin of safety.

Margin of safety, popularised by Benjamin Graham, is the discount of market price below intrinsic value: MOS = (IV − P)/IV. It protects the investor against estimation errors, unexpected events and market volatility. Example: IV ₹250, price ₹180 → MOS 28%. A higher MOS means lower downside risk and higher potential return. In cost accounting, MOS is actual sales minus break-even sales, showing how far sales can fall before losses — e.g. TechNova: ₹1,95,000 cr − ₹1,50,000 cr = ₹45,000 cr (23.08%). Conclusion: MOS converts analysis into a disciplined buying rule.

3.1 Meaning and assumptions of technical analysis

Fundamental analysts ask "what is this company worth?". Technical analysts ask "what is the crowd doing, and what will it probably do next?" They read the footprints of buyers and sellers — price and volume — on charts.

Technical analysis is the study of past market data, chiefly price and volume, through charts and indicators, to forecast future price movements and decide the timing of buying and selling.

Assumptions (basic tenets)

  1. Market value is determined solely by the interaction of demand and supply.
  2. Market discounts everything — all known information (fundamentals, news, psychology) is already reflected in price.
  3. Prices move in trends that persist for appreciable periods; the aim is to identify a trend early and ride it.
  4. Changes in trend are caused by shifts in demand and supply, and these shifts can be detected on charts.
  5. History repeats itself — chart patterns recur because crowd psychology (fear and greed) is repetitive.
  6. Demand and supply are governed by rational and irrational factors, including moods and guesses.

Advantages and limitations

Advantages

  • Identifies entry and exit timing
  • Quick; works on any liquid instrument
  • Captures market psychology
  • Useful signals for short-term traders; stop-loss discipline

Limitations

  • Subjective pattern interpretation
  • False signals (whipsaws) in sideways markets
  • Self-fulfilling at times, then fails
  • Conflicts with the weak form of EMH (past prices should not predict future prices)
  • Ignores intrinsic value

Example 1 (point & figure). Box size ₹10, 3-box reversal. Price rises 200 → 250, then falls to 215, then to 200.

  1. 200 → 250 = five boxes: one X column from 210 to 250.
  2. Fall to 215 = 35 points = 3 full boxes (₹30): a reversal of exactly 3 boxes → new O column from 240 down to 220.
  3. Fall to 200: O column extends to 200. Only moves of ₹10 multiples count; small wiggles are ignored — that is the noise filter.

Example 1A (point & figure with a reversal and a breakout). Box size ₹5, 3-box reversal. Prices move 100 → 118 → 99 → 112 → 126.

  1. 100 → 118: X boxes at 105, 110 and 115. The price of 118 has not reached 120, so the column tops at 115.
  2. 118 → 99: a 3-box reversal from a top of 115 needs a fall to 115 − 15 = 100. The price of 99 is below 100, so a new O column is plotted one box lower: 110, 105, 100.
  3. 99 → 112: a reversal up from 100 needs 100 + 15 = 115. The price of 112 is short of that, so nothing is plotted. The noise is filtered out.
  4. 112 → 126: the price is above 115, so a new X column runs from 105 to 125.
  5. Signal: the new X column (top 125) rises above the previous X column (top 115). This is a double-top breakout, a P&F buy signal. Note that the chart has no time axis; the number of days taken does not matter.

3.4 Dow theory and trend analysis

Dow theory, developed from the writings of Charles H. Dow (founder of The Wall Street Journal) and refined by William Hamilton and Robert Rhea, holds that the market moves in three simultaneous trends — primary, secondary and minor — and that a primary trend is confirmed only when the major averages confirm each other.

The six tenets

  1. The averages discount everything.
  2. The market has three trends:
    • Primary — the main bull or bear trend lasting a year or more (like the tide).
    • Secondary — corrections against the primary trend lasting three weeks to three months, typically retracing one-third to two-thirds of the prior move (like waves).
    • Minor — day-to-day fluctuations of less than three weeks (like ripples); insignificant.
  3. Primary trends have three phases. Bull: accumulation (informed investors buy), public participation (rising earnings, trend followers join), distribution / excess (speculation; informed investors sell). Bear: distribution, public panic, discouraged/despair selling.
  4. The averages must confirm each other — originally the Industrial and Rail (Transport) averages; in India an analyst might check Nifty 50 against broader indices.
  5. Volume must confirm the trend — volume should rise in the direction of the primary trend.
  6. A trend persists until a definite reversal signal — in an uptrend, a failure to make a higher high followed by a break below the previous low.

Trend rules: Uptrend = series of higher highs and higher lows. Downtrend = lower highs and lower lows. Sideways (horizontal) trend = roughly equal highs and lows. A trendline joins at least two (preferably three) lows in an uptrend or highs in a downtrend; its break is a warning of trend change.

Criticisms of Dow theory: signals come late (after much of the move); secondary vs primary is hard to tell in real time; it was designed for averages, not individual stocks; confirmation between averages is subjective.

The Indian market's rally from the March 2020 low showed Dow-like phases: accumulation amid pessimism, then broad participation as earnings recovered and retail investors joined in large numbers (demat accounts surged), then increasingly speculative activity in small-caps.

3.5 Support, resistance, trendlines and chart patterns

Support is a price level at which demand is strong enough to stop a fall (buyers step in). Resistance is a price level at which supply is strong enough to stop a rise (sellers step in). Once decisively broken, resistance tends to become support and support becomes resistance (role reversal).

Why they exist: memory of past prices (investors who bought at ₹500 and saw it fall want to "get out at cost" when it returns to ₹500 → resistance); round numbers (Nifty 20,000); previous highs/lows; moving averages.

3.6 Moving averages: SMA and EMA

A moving average is the average price over the last n periods, recalculated each period, which smooths out fluctuations to reveal the trend. The simple moving average (SMA) weights all n prices equally; the exponential moving average (EMA) gives more weight to recent prices and so reacts faster.

SMAn = (Pt + Pt−1 + … + Pt−n+1) ÷ n
EMAt = α × Pt + (1 − α) × EMAt−1,   α = 2 ÷ (n + 1)

The first EMA is usually seeded with the SMA of the first n prices. Signals: price crossing above its MA = buy; below = sell. Short MA crossing above long MA = golden cross (bullish, classic pair 50-day and 200-day); crossing below = death cross (bearish).

3.7 Relative Strength Index (RSI)

The Relative Strength Index, developed by J. Welles Wilder (1978), is a momentum oscillator ranging from 0 to 100 that compares the size of recent gains with recent losses (usually over 14 periods) to identify overbought and oversold conditions.

RS = Average gain ÷ Average loss (over n periods)     RSI = 100 − 100 ÷ (1 + RS)

RSI > 70 = overbought (possible fall); RSI < 30 = oversold (possible rise); 50 = neutral. Divergence: price makes a new high but RSI makes a lower high → bearish warning (and vice versa). Wilder's original method smooths averages: new avg = (previous avg × 13 + current) ÷ 14; exam problems usually use simple averages as below.

Example 5 (14-day RSI from the price series in Example 3).

  1. Daily changes (days 2–15): +2, −1, +3, +2, −1, +3, +2, −3, +2, +3, −1, +3, −1, +3.
  2. Total gains = 2+3+2+3+2+2+3+3+3 = 23; total losses = 1+1+3+1+1 = 7.
  3. Average gain = 23 ÷ 14 = 1.6429; average loss = 7 ÷ 14 = 0.5.
  4. RS = 1.6429 ÷ 0.5 = 3.2857.
  5. RSI = 100 − 100 ÷ 4.2857 = 100 − 23.33 = 76.67 → overbought; a cautious trader would avoid fresh buying and watch for a reversal signal.

Example 6 (reverse). What RS gives an RSI of exactly 70? 70 = 100 − 100/(1 + RS) ⇒ 100/(1 + RS) = 30 ⇒ 1 + RS = 3.3333 ⇒ RS = 2.33 (average gains 2.33 times average losses).

3.8 Rate of Change (ROC)

Rate of Change is a momentum indicator measuring the percentage change in price between the current period and n periods ago.

ROCn = (Pt − Pt−n) ÷ Pt−n × 100

Oscillates around zero. Positive and rising = strengthening upward momentum; crossing below zero = sell signal; extreme readings = overbought/oversold.

Example 7. From the Example 3 series: 10-day ROC on day 15 compares 116 with day 5's 106. ROC = (116 − 106) ÷ 106 × 100 = 9.43%. 5-day ROC (vs day 10's 109) = 7 ÷ 109 × 100 = 6.42%. Both positive → upward momentum.

3.9 MACD (Moving Average Convergence Divergence)

MACD, developed by Gerald Appel, is a trend-following momentum indicator equal to the difference between a 12-period EMA and a 26-period EMA. A 9-period EMA of MACD is the signal line; MACD minus signal line is the histogram.

MACD = EMA12 − EMA26    Signal = EMA9(MACD)    Histogram = MACD − Signal

Signals: MACD crosses above signal line = buy; below = sell. MACD above zero = short-term average above long-term (bullish trend). Divergence between MACD and price warns of reversal.

Example 8. EMA12 = ₹1,052, EMA26 = ₹1,040, signal line = 10.

  1. MACD = 1,052 − 1,040 = 12.
  2. Histogram = 12 − 10 = +2.
  3. MACD is above zero and above its signal line → bullish; a shrinking histogram on later days would warn of weakening momentum.

3.11 Efficient Market Hypothesis and behavioural finance (enrichment)

The Efficient Market Hypothesis (EMH), associated with Eugene Fama (1970), states that security prices fully reflect all available information, so investors cannot consistently earn abnormal (risk-adjusted) returns.

FormPrices reflectImplicationTests
WeakAll past price and volume informationTechnical analysis cannot earn abnormal returns (random walk)Serial-correlation tests, runs tests, filter-rule tests
Semi-strongAll publicly available informationFundamental analysis of public data cannot earn abnormal returnsEvent studies (earnings announcements, stock splits, bonus issues)
StrongAll information, public and privateEven insiders cannot earn abnormal returnsStudies of insider and mutual-fund manager returns

Behavioural finance explains why prices can deviate from value: overconfidence, herding, loss aversion (Kahneman and Tversky's prospect theory), anchoring, mental accounting, confirmation bias, disposition effect (selling winners too early, holding losers too long). These biases partly explain bubbles, crashes and momentum — the patterns technical analysts try to exploit.

4.1 Concept of equity valuation and intrinsic value

A mango tree is worth the value of all the mangoes it will give you in future, adjusted for the fact that mangoes next year are worth less to you than mangoes today. A share is worth the present value of all the cash it will give its owner.

Intrinsic value (fundamental or true value) of a share is the present value of all future cash benefits (dividends and the eventual selling price) expected from it, discounted at the investor's required rate of return. Equity valuation is the process of estimating this value.

V0 = D1/(1+k) + D2/(1+k)² + … + Dn/(1+k)n + Pn/(1+k)n

V0 = intrinsic value today; Dt = dividend expected in year t; Pn = expected price at the end of year n; k = required rate of return (cost of equity, often from CAPM).

Decision rule and estimation of intrinsic value

ComparisonStatusDecision
Intrinsic value > market priceUnder-valuedBuy / hold
Intrinsic value < market priceOver-valuedSell / avoid
Intrinsic value ≈ market priceFairly valuedHold; no action

Approaches: (1) Absolute / discounted cash flow: dividend discount models, FCFE/FCFF models; (2) Relative: multiples such as P/E, P/B, P/S, EV/EBITDA compared with peers; (3) Asset-based: book value or liquidation value. Good practice is to triangulate using at least two methods.

Example 1 (one-year holding). You expect a dividend of ₹5 and a price of ₹120 in one year; required return 12%.

  1. V0 = (5 + 120) ÷ 1.12 = 125 ÷ 1.12 = ₹111.61.
  2. If the market price is ₹105, the share is under-valued by ₹6.61 → buy.

4.3 Earnings valuation model and P/E ratio analysis

The earnings (capitalisation) model values a share by capitalising its expected earnings at the required rate of return. The price–earnings (P/E) ratio is market price per share divided by earnings per share; it shows how many rupees investors are willing to pay for ₹1 of earnings.

No-growth earnings model: P0 = E1 ÷ k   (when all earnings are paid out, E = D)
With growth opportunities: P0 = E1 ÷ k + PVGO
P/E valuation: Value = Normal/benchmark P/E × Expected EPS
Justified (forward) P/E = (D1/E1) ÷ (k − g) = payout ÷ (k − g)    Justified trailing P/E = payout × (1 + g) ÷ (k − g)

PVGO = present value of growth opportunities — the part of price that comes from future reinvestment at returns above k.

Determinants of P/E: higher growth (g) → higher P/E; higher payout (for a given g) → higher P/E; higher risk (k) → lower P/E; also interest rates, quality of earnings, industry position.

Example 2 (P/E valuation and decision). Expected EPS = ₹20; industry P/E = 18; market price = ₹400.

  1. Value = 18 × 20 = ₹360.
  2. Price ₹400 > ₹360 → over-valued by ₹40 (price implies a P/E of 400 ÷ 20 = 20 vs peers' 18) → avoid unless growth is superior.

Example 3 (justified P/E and PVGO). EPS last year ₹10, payout 40%, ROE 15%, k = 13%.

  1. g = b × ROE = 0.60 × 15% = 9%.
  2. D0 = 0.40 × 10 = ₹4; D1 = 4 × 1.09 = ₹4.36.
  3. P0 = 4.36 ÷ (0.13 − 0.09) = ₹109.
  4. Justified forward P/E = 0.40 ÷ 0.04 = 10×; trailing = 0.40 × 1.09 ÷ 0.04 = 10.9× (check: 109 ÷ 10 = 10.9 ✔).
  5. E1 = 10 × 1.09 = 10.90; no-growth value E1/k = 10.90 ÷ 0.13 = 83.85; PVGO = 109 − 83.85 = ₹25.15. About 23% of the price is paid for growth.

Indian FMCG leaders have historically traded at high P/E multiples because of steady growth and low risk, while PSU banks and metal companies have traded at low P/Es because of cyclicality and asset-quality risk. A low P/E is not automatically "cheap" — General Question 9 asks exactly this.

P/E cannot be used when EPS is negative (the slide shows a meaningless −6.3× for 2007). Use P/S or P/B instead. Also compare P/E only within the same industry.

4.5 Dividend discount models (DDM)

A dividend discount model values a share as the present value of all expected future dividends, because in the long run dividends are the only cash an equity investor receives from the company.

(a) Zero-growth model

P0 = D ÷ k     (reverse: k = D ÷ P0)

Assumes the same dividend D forever (a perpetuity). Suits preference shares and mature companies with constant dividends.

Example 5. Constant dividend ₹12, k = 15%. P0 = 12 ÷ 0.15 = ₹80. Reverse: if the price is ₹80 and dividend ₹12, implied k = 12 ÷ 80 = 15%.

(b) Constant-growth (Gordon) model

P0 = D1 ÷ (k − g) = D0(1 + g) ÷ (k − g)
Reverse: k = D1/P0 + g  (dividend yield + growth)     g = k − D1/P0
Growth estimate: g = b × ROE (b = retention ratio)

Assumptions: dividends grow at a constant rate g forever; k > g; k and g constant; the firm pays dividends. D0 = dividend just paid; D1 = next year's dividend.

Example 6. D0 = ₹4, g = 8%, k = 14%.

  1. D1 = 4 × 1.08 = ₹4.32.
  2. P0 = 4.32 ÷ (0.14 − 0.08) = 4.32 ÷ 0.06 = ₹72.
  3. If the market price is ₹65 → under-valued → buy.

Example 7 (reverse problems).

  1. Find k: price ₹90, D1 ₹4.50, g 9%. k = 4.50 ÷ 90 + 0.09 = 0.05 + 0.09 = 14%.
  2. Find g: price ₹120, D1 ₹6, k 13%. g = 0.13 − 6 ÷ 120 = 0.13 − 0.05 = 8%.

Example 8 (CAPM + Gordon). β = 1.2, Rf = 6.5%, Rm = 13%; D1 = ₹5.50, g = 8%.

  1. k = 6.5 + 1.2 × (13 − 6.5) = 6.5 + 7.8 = 14.3%.
  2. P0 = 5.50 ÷ (0.143 − 0.08) = 5.50 ÷ 0.063 = ₹87.30.

Using D0 instead of D1 in the numerator is the single most common error. If the question says "has just paid" or "last dividend", multiply by (1 + g) first. If g ≥ k, the model does not work — use a multi-stage model.

(c) Multi-stage (two-stage) growth model

P0 = Σt=1..n D0(1+g1)t/(1+k)t + [Dn+1/(k − g2)] ÷ (1+k)n

Stage 1: supernormal growth g1 for n years (discount each dividend). Stage 2: constant growth g2 forever — compute the terminal price Pn with Gordon at the end of year n, then discount it n years.

4.6 Free cash flow to equity (FCFE) model (enrichment)

FCFE is the cash available to equity shareholders after operating expenses, taxes, capital expenditure, working-capital investment and net borrowing. It is used when a company pays little or no dividend but generates cash.

FCFE = Net income + Depreciation − Capex − ΔWorking capital + (New borrowing − Debt repaid)
Equity value = FCFE1 ÷ (ke − g)  (constant growth); per share = equity value ÷ shares

FCFF (free cash flow to the firm) is discounted at WACC to get firm value; subtract debt to get equity value.

Example 11. Net income ₹500 cr, depreciation ₹120 cr, capex ₹300 cr, increase in working capital ₹60 cr, net borrowing ₹80 cr. Growth 6%, ke 14%, 50 crore shares.

  1. FCFE0 = 500 + 120 − 300 − 60 + 80 = ₹340 cr.

  3. Equity value = 360.4 ÷ (0.14 − 0.06) = ₹4,505 cr; per share = 4,505 ÷ 50 = ₹90.10.

5.1 Meaning and objectives of equity portfolio management

A portfolio is a combination of securities held by an investor. Portfolio management is the art and science of selecting the right mix of securities, in the right proportions, and revising it over time, to maximise return for a given level of risk (or minimise risk for a given return) in line with the investor's objectives.

Objectives

  • Capital appreciation (growth)
  • Stable income (dividends)
  • Safety of principal
  • Risk reduction through diversification
  • Liquidity and marketability
  • Tax efficiency
  • Beating a benchmark (e.g. Nifty 50 TRI)

Process

  1. Specify objectives and constraints (investment policy)
  2. Security analysis (Units 2–4)
  3. Portfolio construction (asset allocation, selection, weights)
  4. Portfolio revision (rebalancing, formula plans)
  5. Portfolio evaluation (Sharpe, Treynor, Jensen)

5.4 Diversification and the risk–return trade-off

Diversification is spreading investment across securities whose returns are not perfectly positively correlated, so that the unsystematic risk of individual securities offsets and portfolio risk falls without sacrificing expected return. Equal-weighted N-stock portfolio: σp² = (1/N) × average variance + (1 − 1/N) × average covariance

5.5 Modern Portfolio Theory — Harry Markowitz

Modern Portfolio Theory (MPT), introduced by Harry Markowitz in "Portfolio Selection" (Journal of Finance, 1952) — work for which he shared the 1990 Nobel Prize in Economics — shows that investors should evaluate securities by their contribution to the risk and return of the whole portfolio, measuring return by the mean and risk by the variance, and that diversification among less-than-perfectly correlated assets yields a set of efficient portfolios.

Assumptions

  1. Investors are rational and risk-averse; they prefer more return to less and less risk to more.
  2. Investors base decisions only on expected return and variance (SD) of returns.
  3. Returns are (approximately) normally distributed.
  4. A single-period investment horizon.
  5. Markets are perfect: no taxes or transaction costs; securities are infinitely divisible.
  6. Investors can estimate expected returns, variances and covariances of all securities.

Contribution of Markowitz

  • Quantified risk as variance and showed that portfolio risk depends on covariances, not just individual risks.
  • Replaced naive diversification with mathematically efficient diversification.
  • Introduced the efficient frontier and the principle of dominance.
  • Laid the foundation for CAPM, the single-index model and modern asset allocation.

The efficient frontier is the set of portfolios that give the highest expected return for each level of risk (or the lowest risk for each level of return). A portfolio is efficient if no other portfolio has (a) higher return with the same risk or (b) lower risk with the same return — this is the dominance principle.

Example 6 (dominance). Portfolios: P (12%, 8%), Q (12%, 10%), R (15%, 10%), S (10%, 8%) as (return, SD).

  1. P dominates S (same risk 8%, higher return).
  2. R dominates Q (same risk 10%, higher return).
  3. Efficient set: P and R. Choice between them depends on the investor's risk appetite.

Optimal portfolio: the point where the investor's highest indifference curve (risk–return utility curve) touches the efficient frontier. A conservative investor's optimum lies near the minimum-variance portfolio; an aggressive investor's lies higher up the frontier.

Limitations of Markowitz: enormous data needs (for 100 stocks: 100 returns, 100 variances and 4,950 covariances); estimates are unstable; assumes normal returns; single period; ignores transaction costs. Sharpe's single-index model solves the data problem.

5.6 Capital Asset Pricing Model (CAPM) and the SML

Since unsystematic risk can be diversified away for free, the market pays you only for the risk you cannot diversify — systematic risk, measured by beta. Your required return = risk-free rate + beta × market's reward for risk.

The Capital Asset Pricing Model, developed by William Sharpe (1964), John Lintner (1965) and Jan Mossin (1966), describes the relationship between systematic risk (beta) and the expected (required) return of a security in equilibrium. Graphically this relationship is the Security Market Line (SML).

E(Ri) = Rf + βi [E(Rm) − Rf]

Rf = risk-free rate (e.g. 91-day T-bill or 10-year G-sec yield); E(Rm) = expected market return (Nifty/Sensex); [E(Rm) − Rf] = market risk premium; βi = beta of the security. Portfolio beta βp = Σ wiβi.

Assumptions of CAPM

  1. Investors are risk-averse and hold Markowitz-efficient portfolios.
  2. Homogeneous expectations about returns, variances, covariances.
  3. Unlimited borrowing and lending at the risk-free rate.
  4. No taxes, transaction costs; perfectly divisible assets; perfect competition (price-takers).
  5. Single holding period.
  6. All assets are marketable.

Example 7 (required return and valuation via SML). Rf = 6.5%, Rm = 13% (market risk premium 6.5%). An analyst forecasts returns: X 16% (β 1.2), Y 11% (β 0.8), Z 15.5% (β 1.5).

Beta and systematic risk

Beta is the slope of the characteristic line (Unit 1). It measures systematic risk: systematic variance = βi²σm². Under CAPM only beta is priced; unsystematic risk earns no premium because it can be diversified away. Aggressive stocks β > 1; defensive β < 1.

Applications of CAPM in equity investment decisions

  1. Estimating the required return / cost of equity (ke) used in DDM and DCF valuation (Unit 4 Example 8).
  2. Identifying under- and over-valued securities using the SML.
  3. Measuring risk-adjusted performance (Jensen's alpha, Treynor ratio).
  4. Building portfolios with a target beta (e.g. defensive portfolio for a retiree).
  5. Capital budgeting — project hurdle rates.

Limitations: unrealistic assumptions; beta is unstable and depends on the index and period used; the true market portfolio is unobservable (Roll's critique); empirical tests show factors other than beta (size, value) also explain returns.

Indian analysts commonly use the 10-year Government of India bond yield as Rf and a long-run Nifty/Sensex return to estimate the equity risk premium. Mutual-fund factsheets report scheme beta against the benchmark index.

5.7 Capital Market Line (CML) vs Security Market Line (SML)

CML: E(Rp) = Rf + [(E(Rm) − Rf) ÷ σm] × σp

The CML joins Rf to the market (tangency) portfolio M on the efficient frontier. Points between Rf and M = lending portfolios; beyond M = borrowing portfolios. Its slope is the market's Sharpe ratio (reward per unit of total risk).

Example 9. Rf 6%, Rm 12%, σm 15%.

  1. Slope = (12 − 6) ÷ 15 = 0.4.
  2. Efficient portfolio with σp = 10%: E(R) = 6 + 0.4 × 10 = 10% (≈ 2/3 in M, 1/3 lent at Rf).
  3. With σp = 20%: E(R) = 14% (borrowing to invest 133% in M).
  4. Reverse: to earn 15%, σp = (15 − 6) ÷ 0.4 = 22.5%.

5.8 Sharpe's single-index model (SIM)

Sharpe's single-index model (William Sharpe, 1963) assumes that securities' returns are related to each other only through their common relationship with a single market index. Each security's return is split into a part explained by the market and a part unique to the firm.

Ri = αi + βiRm + ei
Total risk: σi² = βi²σm² (systematic) + σei² (unsystematic / residual)
Covariance between two stocks: Covij = βiβjσm²
Portfolio: βp = Σwiβi; αp = Σwiαi; σp² = βp²σm² + Σwi²σei²

Assumptions

  1. Returns of each security are linearly related to one market index.
  2. The residuals ei have zero mean and are uncorrelated with the index (Cov(ei, Rm) = 0).
  3. Residuals of different securities are uncorrelated (Cov(ei, ej) = 0) — all co-movement comes through the market.
  4. Investors are risk-averse mean-variance optimisers (as in Markowitz).

Relevance in equity portfolio construction

  • Drastically fewer inputs: for n stocks, SIM needs 3n + 2 estimates (n alphas, n betas, n residual variances, plus E(Rm) and σm²) instead of Markowitz's 2n + n(n − 1)/2. For 100 stocks: 302 vs 5,150.
  • Separates systematic from unsystematic risk — shows how much risk diversification can remove.
  • Provides a simple ranking rule for building optimal portfolios (Sharpe's cut-off method, below).
  • Practical: betas are readily available from regression on Nifty/Sensex.

Limitation: ignores industry and other common factors (multi-index models address this).

5.9 Arbitrage Pricing Theory (enrichment)

Arbitrage Pricing Theory (APT), proposed by Stephen Ross (1976), states that a security's expected return is a linear function of several macro-economic factors, and that the absence of arbitrage opportunities enforces this relationship.

E(Ri) = Rf + bi1λ1 + bi2λ2 + … + bikλk

b = sensitivity of the security to factor k; λ = risk premium for factor k (e.g. inflation, industrial production, interest-rate spread).

Example 13. Rf 6%; factor 1 (GDP growth) premium 4% with b = 1.2; factor 2 (inflation) premium 2% with b = 0.5. E(R) = 6 + 1.2 × 4 + 0.5 × 2 = 6 + 4.8 + 1.0 = 11.8%.

CAPM vs APT: CAPM uses one factor (market) and needs the market portfolio; APT allows many factors, needs fewer assumptions (no need for mean-variance investors or the market portfolio), but does not say which factors to use.

5.11 Equity portfolio evaluation

Portfolio evaluation assesses whether a portfolio's return was adequate for the risk taken, and compares it with a benchmark and with other portfolios. Raw returns mislead; risk-adjusted measures are required.

Sharpe ratio (reward-to-variability) = (Rp − Rf) ÷ σp
Treynor ratio (reward-to-volatility) = (Rp − Rf) ÷ βp
Jensen's alpha = Rp − [Rf + βp(Rm − Rf)]
M² (Modigliani) = Rf + Sharpep × σm − Rm  (return difference at market risk)
Information ratio = (Rp − RB) ÷ Tracking error

Sharpe uses total risk — use for an investor's entire (possibly undiversified) portfolio. Treynor and Jensen use beta — use for well-diversified portfolios or funds that are part of a larger portfolio. Higher is better for all.

5.12 Equity investment strategies: active and passive

There are two ways to travel a busy road. You can weave between lanes, hoping to arrive earlier (active). Or you can stay in one lane at the speed of traffic, knowing you will arrive with the crowd (passive). Weaving sometimes wins, but it costs fuel, carries risk, and most drivers who try it do not gain much. Equity strategies make the same choice: try to beat the market, or own the market cheaply.

An active equity strategy tries to earn a return above a benchmark (positive alpha) through security selection, sector allocation or market timing, on the belief that markets are not fully efficient. A passive equity strategy tries to earn the benchmark's return at minimum cost and tracking error, usually by holding the index constituents in index weights (index funds, ETFs), on the belief that markets are largely efficient and that costs decide net returns.

Every portfolio has to choose between active and passive management, and the choice determines costs, risk and how performance is judged (Sharpe/Treynor/Jensen for active managers; tracking error for passive ones). The course plan lists active strategies, passive strategies and emerging trends as separate items, so expect a 5- or 10-mark question.

Q (10 marks). Explain Markowitz's portfolio theory. What is the efficient frontier?

Introduction. Harry Markowitz (1952) founded Modern Portfolio Theory, showing that risk should be judged at portfolio level using mean and variance, and that diversification among imperfectly correlated securities reduces risk.

Assumptions. Rational, risk-averse investors; decisions based on expected return and variance; single period; perfect markets; normal returns; estimable inputs.

Portfolio risk. σp² = Σwi²σi² + ΣΣwiwjCovij. Lower correlation → lower risk (e.g. with ρ = 0.3 a 60:40 mix has σ 10.67% vs weighted-average 13.2%).

Efficient frontier. [Draw the curve.] The set of portfolios offering maximum return for each risk level; lower half is dominated. Dominance principle explained.

Optimal portfolio. Tangency of the investor's highest indifference curve with the frontier.

Contribution. Quantified diversification; introduced covariance; basis of CAPM and asset allocation.

Limitations. Huge input requirement (n(n−1)/2 covariances), unstable estimates, single period, ignores costs.

Conclusion. Markowitz made diversification scientific; Sharpe's SIM and CAPM later simplified and extended it.

GQ2. Mr Rajesh — retired, FD and PPF heavy

Issue. Rajesh's portfolio is "safe" in nominal terms but exposed to two systematic risks that diversification within deposits cannot remove.

Inflation (purchasing-power) risk. The real return is what matters for a retiree's lifestyle. Illustration (assumed figures): FD rate 7%, tax slab 30% → post-tax 4.9%; inflation 6% → real return = 1.049 ÷ 1.06 − 1 = −1.04%. His wealth is shrinking in real terms every year, and medical inflation for seniors is typically higher than headline CPI.

(ii) Suitable alternatives (conservative, inflation-aware).

  1. Senior Citizens' Savings Scheme (SCSS) — government-backed, quarterly interest, higher than regular FD rates; investment limit ₹30 lakh.
  2. RBI Floating Rate Savings Bonds — rate resets every six months linked to the NSC rate, protecting against rising rates.
  3. Short-duration / target-maturity debt funds and G-secs via RBI Retail Direct — diversify issuers and maturities (a bond ladder reduces reinvestment risk).
  4. Equity exposure of 20–30% through large-cap index funds, dividend-yield or balanced advantage funds — the only mainstream asset that has historically beaten inflation over long periods.
  5. Gold 5–10% (gold ETFs / Sovereign Gold Bonds in the secondary market) — hedge against inflation and rupee depreciation.

  7. Systematic Withdrawal Plan (SWP) from hybrid funds for regular, tax-efficient cash flow; keep 1–2 years' expenses in FDs/liquid funds as an emergency bucket.

Suggested structure ("bucket strategy"). Bucket 1 (0–2 years' needs): FDs, liquid funds, SCSS. Bucket 2 (3–7 years): debt funds, floating rate bonds. Bucket 3 (8+ years): equity and gold for growth.

Risks to the plan. Equity volatility (keep allocation modest; use SIPs), credit risk in debt funds (prefer government/AAA), liquidity (avoid locking everything).

Conclusion. By shifting a portion from fixed-rate deposits to floating-rate, equity and gold assets, Rajesh keeps a conservative profile while protecting purchasing power.

  5. Portfolio construction: take exposure through "picks and shovels" (transmission, equipment) as well as developers, and limit sector weight to control concentration risk.

Conclusion. A structural theme deserves a place in a long-term portfolio, but selection should be based on fundamentals and valuation, not recent price momentum.

GQ7. Regulatory changes in technology + global slowdown

Regulatory changes in technology and a global slowdown have raised volatility, reduced confidence and caused sharp declines in several established companies. Critically evaluate: (i) impact on the risk–return profile of equity; (ii) role of qualitative analysis in assessing resilience; (iii) whether the environment favours short-term trading or long-term investing.

Model answer

(i) Impact on risk and return.

  • Systematic risk rises: the slowdown lowers expected earnings across the market; volatility (India VIX) rises; investors demand a higher risk premium, so the discount rate k rises and valuations fall (P = D1/(k − g): higher k, lower g → lower P).
  • Sector-specific (unsystematic) risk rises for technology: regulation (data-protection, platform, antitrust rules) can cap growth, add compliance cost or change business models.
  • Expected return going forward can rise for new buyers because prices have fallen — but with higher uncertainty; correlations tend to rise in crises, reducing diversification benefits.

(ii) Role of qualitative analysis. Numbers are backward-looking; in uncertainty the key question is resilience. Assess: quality and integrity of management; competitive moat (brand, switching costs, network effects); diversification of revenue across geographies/clients; balance-sheet strength (cash, low debt); regulatory relationships and compliance culture; governance (independent board, disclosure quality under LODR); adaptability and R&D. Firms with strong qualitative profiles recover faster.

(iii) Short-term trading vs long-term investing. High volatility tempts short-term trading, but whipsaws, gaps and leverage make it risky and costly for most investors. For long-term investors, sharp declines in fundamentally strong companies can create margin-of-safety opportunities. Recommended stance: long-term investing, entering gradually (SIP/rupee cost averaging), focusing on quality companies with strong balance sheets, maintaining diversification and some cash; avoid leverage. Short-term trading suits only experienced traders with strict stop-losses.

Conclusion. Uncertainty raises risk but also future expected returns; disciplined, quality-focused long-term investing is the more rational response.

GQ8. Central bank raises rates; consumer discretionary hit, banks resilient

A sudden rise in interest rates with inflation concerns has caused markets to fall sharply; consumer discretionary stocks are hit hardest while some banking and financial services stocks are resilient. Critically evaluate: (i) how monetary policy changes affect the risk and return of different sectors; (ii) significance of macro-economic analysis in portfolio management during tightening; (iii) whether the scenario offers value-investing opportunities or warrants caution.

Model answer

(i) Transmission to sectors. Contractionary policy raises borrowing costs and the discount rate for all stocks (systematic effect) and cuts demand for credit-financed purchases.

SectorEffect of rate hike
Consumer discretionary (autos, durables, retail, travel)Demand depends on EMIs and disposable income → falls; high-P/E growth stocks lose most because their value lies in distant cash flows (long "equity duration")

(ii) Significance of macro analysis. Macro (economy) analysis — the "E" of EIC — tells the manager which way the tide is moving: inflation trend, RBI stance, liquidity, yield curve, growth outlook. It guides sector rotation (towards defensives and beneficiaries), asset allocation (more short-duration debt, less long-duration), beta management (lower portfolio beta), and stress-testing of valuations using a higher k.

(iii) Value opportunity or caution? Both, selectively. Sharp falls can push quality companies below intrinsic value — a value investor re-estimates intrinsic value using the higher required return and buys only with a margin of safety. Caution is needed because (a) further hikes may come if inflation persists; (b) earnings estimates may still be too high (value traps); (c) highly leveraged firms face real distress. GQ9. Rate hike 2.5% → 4.0%; sector falls; VIX 18 → 25; P/E 10 vs 15

The central bank raised rates from 2.5% to 4.0%; consumer discretionary stocks fell 12% while banking fell only 3%; the volatility index rose from 18 to 25. Critically evaluate: (i) implications of rate hikes for sector risk and return; (ii) role of macro analysis and quantitative data in building a resilient portfolio; (iii) with banking P/E 10 vs consumer discretionary P/E 15, should investors favour banks for value investing?

Model answer

Quantify the case first.

  1. Rate increase = 4.0 − 2.5 = 1.5 percentage points = 150 basis points (a 60% rise in the policy rate level).
  2. Relative sensitivity: consumer discretionary fell 12% vs banks 3% → discretionary reacted 4 times as strongly.
  3. Volatility index change = (25 − 18) ÷ 18 = +38.9% → markedly higher expected volatility and fear.
  4. Earnings yields: banks 1/10 = 10%; consumer discretionary 1/15 = 6.67%. Against a 4% policy rate, banks offer a wider equity risk premium.

(i) Implications. Rate hikes raise k for all equities (systematic risk up, as the VIX shows). Discretionary demand is credit- and sentiment-sensitive, and its higher P/E implies more value in distant cash flows, so it falls more (higher effective beta to rates). Banks can gain NIM from repricing loans, so they fall less — lower risk, and their return prospects may improve.

(ii) Macro + quantitative data. Macro analysis identifies the tightening cycle and likely path of rates/inflation; quantitative data (sector betas, rate sensitivities, VIX, earnings yields, P/E vs history, correlations) lets the manager size positions: tilt towards lower-beta and rate-beneficiary sectors, reduce portfolio beta, diversify across uncorrelated assets, and set stop-loss/rebalancing rules. The evidence here (−3% vs −12%) is exactly the kind of data that supports an overweight on financials during tightening.

(iii) Should investors favour banks? Qualified yes. A P/E of 10 with 10% earnings yield and proven resilience suggests value. But a low P/E alone can be a value trap: check (a) P/B vs ROE (for banks P/B is the key multiple — justified P/B = (ROE − g)/(k − g)); (b) asset quality (gross/net NPAs, provisioning); (c) NIM trend and deposit growth; (d) capital adequacy; (e) exposure to rate-sensitive borrowers who may default later in the cycle. Consumer discretionary at P/E 15 may become attractive once rates peak. Recommendation: overweight quality banks with strong deposits and low NPAs now; build discretionary positions gradually as inflation eases.

GQ10. Tech stock +50%, retail −20%, market +8%

After a technology innovation, a leading tech stock rose from $200 to $300 (50%) in three months, while traditional retail fell from $50 to $40 (−20%); the market returned 8%. Revenue growth: tech 30%, retail 5%. Critically evaluate: (i) impact of disruption on risk and return of both sectors; (ii) importance of fundamental metrics (revenue growth, margins) in judging sustainability; (iii) growth opportunity or overvaluation caution?

Model answer

Quantify.

  1. Tech return = (300 − 200) ÷ 200 = 50%; excess over market = 50 − 8 = +42 points.
  2. Retail return = (40 − 50) ÷ 50 = −20%; excess = −20 − 8 = −28 points.
  3. If sales per share grew 30% while price rose 50%, the price-to-sales multiple expanded by 1.50 ÷ 1.30 − 1 = 15.4%; for retail, 0.80 ÷ 1.05 − 1 = −23.8% (multiple contraction). So much of tech's gain is re-rating (expectations), not just growth.

(i) Risk and return. Tech: higher expected growth and returns, but higher valuation risk, volatility (beta), competitive and regulatory risk — expectations are now high, so disappointments are punished. Retail: disruption raises business risk (loss of market share, obsolete formats), lowers growth; but lower valuations may already price in much bad news — potential value if the company adapts (omni-channel).

(ii) Fundamental metrics. Revenue growth of 30% vs 5% supports a higher valuation for tech, but sustainability depends on: profit margins and operating leverage (is growth profitable?); cash flow conversion; customer retention and pricing power; R&D efficiency; balance-sheet strength; P/E relative to growth (PEG ratio = P/E ÷ growth rate — below about 1 is generally considered reasonable). For retail: same-store sales, margin trend, inventory turnover, debt levels.

(iii) Opportunity or caution? A structural innovation can justify long-term growth investing, but a 50% move in three months — faster than revenue growth — signals that sentiment has run ahead of fundamentals. View: invest in the tech leader only after estimating intrinsic value (DCF/multi-stage DDM with realistic growth fade) and on dips offering a margin of safety; stagger purchases; avoid concentration. For retail, look selectively for firms adapting successfully rather than avoiding the whole sector.

Conclusion. Disruption reallocates value between sectors, but price momentum is not proof of value — fundamentals and valuation must confirm it.

Question bank 2 — short answers (2–3 marks)

1. Define investment in economic and financial sense.

Economic: net addition to the capital stock of the country. Financial: allocating money to assets to gain profit over time. Savings flow as financial investment into markets and become economic investment.

2. What is arbitrage? Give an Indian example.

Simultaneous buying in a cheaper market and selling in a dearer market to profit from a temporary price difference in the same asset — e.g. a share quoted lower on NSE than BSE, or cash–futures arbitrage used by arbitrage mutual funds.

3. Distinguish internal and external business risk.

Internal: arises from operational efficiency (cost control, management). External: arises from operating conditions imposed by the environment (policy, competition, demand).

4. What is purchasing-power risk? Name its two causes.

Variation in real returns due to loss of purchasing power of money; caused by demand-pull and cost-push inflation.

5. State the formula for HPR.

HPR = [D + (P1 − P0)]/P0.

6. What does a negative covariance indicate?

Returns move in opposite directions; combining the securities reduces portfolio risk strongly.

7. Name the three depositories/clearing institutions in India's equity market.

Depositories NSDL and CDSL; clearing corporations such as NSE Clearing Ltd and Indian Clearing Corporation Ltd.

8. What are leading indicators? Give two.

Indicators that change before the economy does: monetary policy, fiscal policy, rainfall, capital investment, stock prices.

9. List Porter's five forces.

Threat of new entrants, bargaining power of suppliers, bargaining power of buyers, threat of substitutes, rivalry among existing competitors.

10. Define margin of safety (Graham).

(Intrinsic value − market price)/intrinsic value — the cushion against analysis errors.

11. State four assumptions of technical analysis.

Market value determined by demand and supply; market discounts everything; prices move in trends; history repeats itself.

12. What is a doji?

A candle whose open and close are nearly equal, signalling indecision and possible reversal.

13. What is a golden cross?

A short-term moving average (e.g. 50-day) crossing above a long-term one (e.g. 200-day) — a bullish signal.

14. State the zero-growth model.

P0 = D/k — value of a constant perpetual dividend.

15. What is the efficient frontier?

The set of portfolios giving maximum expected return for each level of risk; all other portfolios are dominated.

16. Define Jensen's alpha.

α = Rp − [Rf + β(Rm − Rf)]: return earned above the CAPM-required return; positive alpha = superior performance.

17. What is Roll's critique?

The true market portfolio of all assets cannot be observed, so CAPM cannot be tested precisely; using an index as proxy may mislead.

Question bank 3 — long answers and case studies (with full model answers)

L1 (10 marks). Explain the investment process and the objectives and constraints of an investor.

Introduction. Investment is the employment of funds on assets to earn income or capital appreciation. A disciplined process ensures decisions match goals.

Objectives. Return (income and growth), safety of principal, liquidity, hedge against inflation, tax efficiency, capital appreciation for long-term goals.

Constraints. Liquidity needs, time horizon, tax position, legal/regulatory limits (e.g. for trusts, insurers), unique circumstances (health, dependants, ethical preferences).

Process. (1) Investment policy — determine investible surplus, risk tolerance, objectives, constraints and asset classes. (2) Security analysis — fundamental (EIC, ratios) and technical (charts, indicators). (3) Valuation — intrinsic value vs market price (DDM, P/E). (4) Portfolio construction — asset allocation, diversification (Markowitz), selection. (5) Evaluation and revision — Sharpe/Treynor/Jensen, rebalancing, formula plans.

Example. A 28-year-old IT professional with a 25-year horizon and high risk tolerance may hold 75% equity (index + active funds), 20% debt (PPF, EPF) and 5% gold, and review annually.

Conclusion. The process converts savings into goal-based, risk-controlled wealth creation and is a continuous loop.

L2 (10 marks). Discuss the macro-economic factors considered in economy analysis.

Introduction. Economy analysis studies macro factors that affect all companies' earnings and valuations.

  1. GDP growth — higher growth lifts sales and profits.
  2. Savings and investment — fund capital formation and market demand.
  3. Inflation — moderate is fine; high raises costs and interest rates.
  4. Interest rates / monetary policy — RBI's repo decisions change cost of capital and discount rates; expansionary vs contractionary policy.
  5. Budget and fiscal policy — capex allocations, subsidies, fiscal deficit.
  6. Tax structure — corporate tax cut of 2019 raised post-tax earnings.
  7. BoP and exchange rate — rupee moves help exporters or importers.
  8. Monsoon/agriculture — rural demand.
  9. Infrastructure and political stability.

Forecasting tools: leading, coincident and lagging indicators; surveys; diffusion indices; econometric models; business cycle phase analysis [draw the cycle].

Conclusion. A favourable macro environment is the first filter of the EIC framework.

L3 (10 marks). Explain company analysis and the role of ratio analysis in it.

Introduction. Company analysis evaluates an individual firm to estimate the present and future value of its shares.

Qualitative: competitive edge, market share, management quality, governance, business model, R&D, labour relations.

Quantitative: growth and stability of sales, earnings growth, and ratio analysis:

  • Liquidity — current ratio, quick ratio.
  • Leverage — debt–equity, interest coverage.
  • Efficiency — asset, inventory turnover, DSO.
  • Profitability — margins, ROA, ROE, ROCE; DuPont decomposition.
  • Market — EPS, P/E, P/B, dividend yield.

Tools: comparative, common-size and trend statements; cash-flow analysis.

Illustration. Sahyadri Foods: CR 2.0, D/E 0.4, ICR 6×, NPM 12.5%, ROE 20% (= 12.5% × 0.923 × 1.733), P/E 12 — financially sound, reasonably valued.

Limitations of ratios: based on historical data; accounting policy differences; window dressing; need industry benchmarks.

Conclusion. Ratios turn financial statements into comparable signals of strength, but must be combined with qualitative judgement.

L4 (10 marks). What is technical analysis? Compare it with fundamental analysis.

Introduction. Technical analysis studies past price and volume to forecast price movements and time trades.

Assumptions: demand–supply determine value; market discounts everything; trends persist; history repeats.

Tools: charts (line, bar, P&F, candlestick), patterns (candlestick, head and shoulders, double tops, triangles), Dow theory, support/resistance, indicators (SMA, EMA, RSI, ROC, MACD), breadth and volume.

Comparison: [reproduce the table in Section 3.10: question answered, data, tools, horizon, assumption, users, cause vs effect, EMH challenge.]

Conclusion. They are complementary — fundamental analysis tells what to buy, technical analysis when to buy.

L5 (10 marks). Explain moving averages, RSI, ROC and MACD with formulas and signals.

SMA = average of last n closes; EMA = αPt + (1 − α)EMAt−1 with α = 2/(n + 1). Signals: price/MA crossovers, golden and death crosses.

RSI = 100 − 100/(1 + RS), RS = avg gain/avg loss (14 periods). > 70 overbought, < 30 oversold; divergences warn of reversal. Example: avg gain 1.6429, avg loss 0.5 → RSI 76.67.

ROC = (Pt − Pt−n)/Pt−n × 100. Zero-line crossings and extremes. Example: 116 vs 106 → 9.43%.

MACD = EMA12 − EMA26; signal = 9-EMA of MACD; histogram = difference. Crossovers and zero-line.

Limitations: lagging (MAs), false signals in sideways markets; combine with volume and trend.

L6 (10 marks). Explain Sharpe's single-index model — assumptions, risk decomposition and relevance.

Model: Ri = αi + βiRm + ei. Assumptions: linear relation with one index; residuals zero-mean, uncorrelated with the index and with each other.

Risk: σi² = βi²σm² + σei². Example: β 1.3, σm 15, σe 12 → 380.25 + 144 = 524.25; σ = 22.9%, 72.5% systematic. Portfolio: σp² = βp²σm² + Σw²σe²; the second term shrinks with diversification.

Relevance: needs 3n + 2 inputs instead of n(n + 3)/2; for 100 stocks 302 vs 5,150; betas readily estimated; cut-off method gives optimal weights (Example 12: A 89.47%, B 5.26%, C 5.26%).

Limitations: ignores industry co-movement; relies on stable betas.

L7 (10 marks). Explain the measures of portfolio performance evaluation.

Raw return ignores risk; evaluation needs risk-adjusted measures. Sharpe (Rp − Rf)/σp — total risk, CML-based. Treynor (Rp − Rf)/βp — systematic risk, SML-based. Jensen's alpha Rp − CAPM return — absolute excess return. M² — Sharpe expressed in % at market risk. Information ratio — active return per unit tracking error. Worked comparison (Example 16): Fund Beta leads on Sharpe (0.583) and Treynor (7.78) while Gamma leads on Jensen (2.0%). Choice of measure depends on whether the portfolio is the investor's whole wealth (Sharpe) or part of a diversified holding (Treynor/Jensen). Conclude with SEBI's TRI benchmarking requirement ensuring fair comparison in India.

L8 (15-mark case). Priya, 26, earns ₹1.2 lakh a month, has no dependants and wants to build a corpus for a house in 8 years and retirement in 30 years. She holds only a savings account. Advise her.

1. Issue. Idle savings suffer purchasing-power risk; she needs goal-based allocation.

2. Theory applied. Investment process; risk–return trade-off; Markowitz diversification; rupee cost averaging.

3. Emergency fund. 6 months' expenses in a liquid fund/sweep FD.

4. Goal 1 — house in 8 years (medium horizon): about 50% equity (large-cap/index, hybrid) and 50% debt (short-duration funds, PPF partly); reduce equity progressively as the goal nears (glide path).

5. Goal 2 — retirement in 30 years: 75–80% equity via monthly SIPs in Nifty 50 index fund, flexi-cap and some mid-cap; 15–20% EPF/PPF/NPS; 5% gold ETF/SGB.

6. Risk management. Diversify across market caps and asset classes; term insurance and health insurance before investing; avoid F&O speculation.

7. Numbers. Illustration: a ₹30,000 monthly SIP, if it earned 11% a year, would grow substantially through compounding; the key message is start early and stay invested (see compounding example in Unit 1).

8. Monitoring. Annual review with Sharpe ratio and benchmark comparison; rebalance to target weights (constant ratio plan).

Conclusion. A disciplined, diversified, SIP-based plan balances her high risk capacity with specific goal horizons.

L9 (15-mark case). A director of a listed company buys shares a week before the company announces an unexpected acquisition; the share price jumps 18% after the announcement. Analyse under SEBI regulations and market-efficiency theory.

1. Issue. Possible insider trading — trading while in possession of Unpublished Price Sensitive Information (UPSI).

2. Regulation. SEBI (Prohibition of Insider Trading) Regulations, 2015 prohibit insiders from trading on UPSI; companies must close the trading window around price-sensitive events and maintain a structured digital database of persons with UPSI; insiders must disclose trades. SEBI's quasi-judicial powers allow investigation, disgorgement of profits, penalties and market bans.

3. Market efficiency. The 18% post-announcement jump shows the market is at most semi-strong efficient — public news is priced quickly, but private information was not (strong form fails), letting an insider earn abnormal returns.

4. Impact. Erodes investor confidence, widens spreads, raises cost of capital; unfair to outside shareholders.

5. Recommendations. Board should investigate and report to exchanges; strengthen compliance officer role, pre-clearance of trades, training; investors should favour companies with strong governance (a qualitative factor in company analysis).

Conclusion. Insider trading regulation protects market integrity — SEBI's primary mandate.

Common mistakes and examiner traps

  1. Using D0 instead of D1 in the Gordon model. "Just paid" means D0; multiply by (1 + g).
  2. Forgetting the dividend in HPR or forgetting to annualise multi-year HPRs when comparing.
  3. Dividing by n vs n − 1 without stating which — always state it.
  4. Taking square root of variance too early in portfolio problems — add variance and covariance terms first, then take the root.
  5. Forgetting the "2" in 2wAwBCov (and in each pair for three assets).
  6. Using percentages and decimals together (σ = 10 with Cov = 0.0054). Keep one unit system throughout.
  7. Calling a correlation above 1 or below −1 valid — it signals an arithmetic error.
  8. Classifying interest-rate risk as unsystematic. Your slides place it under systematic risk.
  9. Confusing hammer and hanging man — same shape; the prior trend decides.
  10. Reading RSI 75 as "strong buy" — it means overbought; caution.
  11. Using the SML axis (β) for the CML or vice versa.
  12. Treating low P/E as automatically cheap — check growth, ROE, asset quality (value trap).
  13. Using P/E for loss-making firms — use P/S or P/B.
  14. Applying Gordon when g ≥ k — use a multi-stage model.
  15. Ranking funds only by raw return — always risk-adjust (Sharpe/Treynor/Jensen).
  16. Omitting the decision in numerical answers — end with "buy/avoid", "prefer A", "under-valued".
  17. Writing margin of safety without saying which kind (Graham vs break-even).
  18. Stating that diversification removes all risk — only unsystematic risk.

One-page cheat sheet

Unit 1 — basics

Investment = funds on assets for income/appreciation. Speculation = short-term risk for price gain. Gambling = chance. Arbitrage = buy low/sell high simultaneously.
Systematic: market (Jack Francis; tangible/intangible), interest-rate, purchasing-power (demand-pull, cost-push). Unsystematic: business (internal/external), financial (EBIT→EBT), liquidity.

Unit 1 — maths

HPR = (D + P1 − P0)/P0. Annualise (1+HPR)1/n − 1. E(R) = Σ pR; σ² = Σ p(R − E)². CV = σ/E. Cov = Σ(dA·dB)/n. r = Cov/σσ. β = Cov/σm² = rσi/σm; α = Ȳ − βX̄. Real = (1+n)/(1+i) − 1.

SEBI & market

SEBI 1988, statutory 1992. Quasi-legislative / executive / judicial. ICDR (IPO), LODR (listing), PIT (insider, UPSI), SAST (takeover), MF Regs 1996. NSDL, CDSL; T+1; Sensex 30 (1978–79 = 100), Nifty 50 (1995 = 1000); free-float.

Unit 2 — EIC

Economy: GDP, savings, inflation, rates, budget, tax. Indicators: leading (policy, rainfall), coincident (GDP, IIP), lagging (unemployment, CPI). Industry: growth/cyclical/defensive/cyclical-growth; life cycle pioneering→expansion→stagnation→decay; Porter 5 forces. Company: moat, market share, sales growth & stability, management.

Unit 2 — ratios

CR CA/CL; QR (CA − Inv)/CL; D/E; TIE EBIT/Int; ATO Sales/TA; DSO Rec/(Sales/365); NPM; ROA; ROE; ROCE EBIT/CE. DuPont ROE = NPM × ATO × TA/E. P/E, P/B, P/S, DY, EY. g = b × ROE. MOS = (IV − P)/IV or (Actual − BE)/Actual.

Unit 3 — charts & candles

Line (closes), bar (OHLC), P&F (X/O, no time), candle (body + shadows). Single: Marubozu, Doji, Spinning top, Hammer (bottom, bullish), Hanging man (top, bearish), Shooting star (top, bearish). Multi: Engulfing, Harami, Piercing (bull), Dark cloud (bear), Morning star (bull), Evening star (bear). Gaps: common, breakaway, runaway, exhaustion.

Unit 3 — Dow & indicators

Primary (tide, >1 yr), secondary (waves, 3 wk–3 mo, 1/3–2/3 retrace), minor (ripples). Bull phases: accumulation, public participation, distribution. Averages and volume confirm. SMA; EMA α = 2/(n+1). RSI = 100 − 100/(1+RS): >70 OB, <30 OS. ROC = (Pt/Pt−n − 1)×100. MACD = EMA12 − EMA26; signal 9.

Unit 4 — valuation

IV > P → buy. Zero growth D/k. Gordon D1/(k − g); k = D1/P + g. Two-stage: PV stage-1 D + PV[Dn+1/(k − g2)]. P = E1/k + PVGO. Justified P/E = payout/(k − g); P/B = (ROE − g)/(k − g). Target = P/E × EPSfwd. FCFE = NI + Dep − Capex − ΔWC + net debt.

Unit 5 — portfolio

Rp = Σw R. σp² = wA²σA² + wB²σB² + 2wAwBρσAσB. MVP wA = (σB² − Cov)/(σA² + σB² − 2Cov). ρ = −1 → wA = σB/(σA+σB). N stocks: var/N + (1 − 1/N)cov. Markowitz 1952: mean-variance, efficient frontier, dominance.

Unit 5 — CAPM, SIM, evaluation

CAPM Rf + β(Rm − Rf); above SML = under-valued. CML uses σ; SML uses β. SIM: R = α + βRm + e; σ² = β²σm² + σe²; 3n+2 inputs. Sharpe (R − Rf)/σ; Treynor /β; Jensen R − CAPM; M²; IR = active/TE. Formula plans: constant rupee, constant ratio, rupee cost averaging.

Exam-day checklist

  • Calculator (check battery), two pens, pencil and ruler for diagrams, hall ticket.
  • Read the whole paper first (5 minutes); choose internal-choice questions where you can show a number or a diagram.
  • Time plan for 50 marks: Section A ~30 min, Section B ~40 min, Section C ~30 min (as in the exam-pattern table), 10 min review. The course documents do not state the ESE duration, so confirm it on your hall ticket; this plan fits a two-hour paper.
  • Weighting: about 40 of the 50 marks come from Units 4 and 5 (CO3–CO5). If you must choose where to spend extra minutes, spend them on the valuation and portfolio questions.
  • Every numerical: formula → substitution → table → answer with % or ₹ → one-line decision.
  • Every theory answer: definition → headings → Indian example → conclusion. Draw at least one diagram in each 10-mark answer (EIC funnel, life cycle, candlestick, Dow phases, efficient frontier, SML, diversification curve).
  • Case study: identify the issue, name the theory, use the case's numbers (percentage change, real return, earnings yield, excess return), answer each sub-part under its own heading, recommend and conclude.
  • Check units (percent vs decimal), D0 vs D1, n vs n − 1, and that correlation lies between −1 and +1.
